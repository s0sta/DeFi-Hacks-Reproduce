# Analyses — Volume 07

## SushiSwap (RouteProcessor2) — 2023-04-10 — $3.3M+
*source slug: miso-rekt (500 — no MISO-specific article exists) → resolved to sushi-yoink-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + 13 other chains; token "yoink" (arbitrary-address transfer) through Sushi's 4-day-old RouteProcessor2 router contract, via a fake Uniswap V3 pool callback.
- Root cause: `processRoute()` in RouteProcessor2 (0x044b75f554b886A065b9567891e45c79542d7357 on ETH) accepted arbitrary user data and did not verify the swap counterparty: during `uniswapV3SwapCallback` the router trusted the caller-supplied pool address, so an attacker could impersonate a V3 pool and trigger `safeTransferFrom` with an arbitrary `from` address. 0xfoobar: "line 328 comment is correct, line 340 does not check the pool deployer… no-op swap, call safeTransferFrom on an arbitrary ERC20 and arbitrary from address."
- Mechanics: The attacker created a fake Univ3 pool and inserted its own contract address in place of a genuine liquidity pool in the route. During the callback the router did a no-op swap and then transferred tokens out of any address that had approved RouteProcessor2 — a contract only 4 days old, so relatively few approvers. ~$3.3M was drained across 14 chains (example tx 0xea3480f1…), including 1,800 ETH from DeFi villain 0xSifu. BlockSec and other whitehats raced to drain vulnerable users first and return funds; one user's whitehat attempt on 0xSifu was botched and only 100 ETH was returned.
- Lesson: In any router/aggregator, external callbacks (Uniswap-V3-style `swapCallback`) must verify the pool's identity/deployer before trusting it — hunt for "no-op swap + arbitrary safeTransferFrom" patterns in `processRoute`-style functions. A freshly deployed router is an approval-harvest honeypot: audit the approval surface of every new router version, because time-to-exploit here was only 4 days.
- Aftermath: Head Chef Jared Grey acknowledged the bug, urged users to revoke approvals, and promised a postmortem. rekt framed it as an embarrassment rather than an existential threat, adding that Sushi's drama (SEC subpoena for Grey, paltry bug bounty, $500k salary criticism) continues.

## Mixin Network — 2023-09-25 — $200M
*source slug: mix-rekt (500) → resolved to mixin-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Mixin mainnet (BTC/ETH/stablecoins); Web2 cloud-database breach — leaked private keys of user hot wallets, drained via simple transfers. Not a smart-contract exploit.
- Root cause: Centralized custody of user keys inside a third-party cloud service provider's database; the database was hacked and the stored keys were read directly. Mixin's "decentralised" claim collapsed — the infra failure was entirely web2.
- Mechanics: On Saturday (announced Monday morning) the attackers used the leaked keys to make plain transfers out of hot wallets: 60k ETH (~$94M) to 0x52E86988bd07447C596e9B0C7765F8500113104c, USDT swapped to unfreezable DAI (~$23.5M) via 0x3B5fb9d9da3546e9CE6E5AA3CCEca14C8D20041e, 891 BTC (~$23M) to bc1qq7uefmz6nng5c4dzs9mwrxxyh9sxg5cjg85hes, plus a dispersal address that gas-funded victim addresses to move their USDT. ~$200M total — the biggest hack of 2023 at the time, topping Euler's $197M — with ~$50M still unaccounted for in the write-up. A livestream told users only 50% of assets were guaranteed.
- Lesson: "Decentralized" protocols whose signing keys live server-side are custodians in disguise — the hunt target is where keys are stored, backed up, and who can touch the DB. When funds drain as simple transfers from many addresses (not contract calls), the vector is key exfiltration, not logic — pivot the investigation to infra/opsec immediately.
- Aftermath: Mixin blamed the cloud provider (in contact with Google and SlowMist) and offered a $20M bounty for return; rekt noted the hallmarks of a Lazarus heist and the suspiciously quick swap of USDT into unfreezable DAI.

## MonoX — 2021-11-30 — $31.4M
- Chain/Attack vector: Polygon + Ethereum; price manipulation of the native MONO token through a pricing bug in the Monoswap contract's `swapTokenForExactToken` path, then cashing out overvalued MONO for the rest of the single-token-liquidity pools.
- Root cause: In `swapTokenForExactToken` / `_updateTokenInfo` there was no check preventing the same asset from being used as both tokenIn and tokenOut. Self-swapping MONO→MONO let the price of tokenOut overwrite the price of tokenIn each iteration, pumping MONO's recorded price over many "swaps" without any real demand.
- Mechanics: The attacker looped MONO self-swaps to inflate the token's price to "sky high", then used the overvalued MONO to buy out virtually all other deposited assets in MonoX's vCASH virtual-pair pools. ~$19.4M was taken on Polygon first; 17 minutes later the identical vector hit Ethereum for ~$12M more — total $31.4M (5.7M MATIC, 3.9k WETH, 36.1 WBTC, 5.1M DUCK, 4.1k MIM, 3.1k GHST, 1.2k LINK, 274 IMX). The protocol was ~1 month old and had only passed $30M TVL three days earlier.
- Lesson: Every DEX swap/price-update path must enforce tokenIn != tokenOut (or treat self-swaps as no-ops) — grep for missing same-asset guards in `swapForExact`/`updatePrice` functions of novel AMM designs. Both Halborn and Peckshield had audited Monoswap and both missed it: single-sided/virtual-pair pool pricing deserves a dedicated adversarial pass.
- Aftermath: The bug was identified by BlockSec; the team's Telegram/Twitter statement begged the attacker to "PLEASE reach out to us". rekt flagged the irony of the "streamlined swap routing" sales pitch.

## Moola Market — 2022-10-19 — $8.4M
*source slug: moola-rekt (500) → resolved to moola-markets-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Celo; price manipulation of native token MOO used as lending collateral — no flash loan, "didn't require any coding". First Celo protocol on the leaderboard (#63).
- Root cause: MOO, the protocol's own native token, was accepted as collateral while its only price source was the thin-liquidity Ubeswap pair — trivially pumpable in a bear market, letting a small bag borrow against wildly inflated collateral value.
- Mechanics: With initial funding of 243k CELO (~$180k) the attacker supplied 60k CELO and borrowed 1.8M MOO, then used the remaining CELO to buy MOO on Ubeswap, pumping the price of the MOO collateral. Against the inflated collateral they borrowed out the rest of the protocol: 8.8M CELO ($6.5M), 765k cEUR ($0.7M), 1.8M MOO ($0.6M) and 644k cUSD ($0.6M). Six hours later over 90% of the funds were returned to the Moola multisig; the exploiter kept ~$525k as a bounty and donated 50k CELO ($37k) of it to Impact Market.
- Lesson: A protocol's own token as collateral on its own market, priced by one thin DEX pair, is a standing price-manipulation target — check the ratio of pumpable collateral liquidity vs. borrowable assets. Low-liquidity bear markets make these pumps cheaper; this followed Mango Markets' collateral-manipulation exploit the week before.
- Aftermath: Moola appealed publicly for return with a bounty; the team subsequently proposed removing MOO as collateral via governance. rekt: "a guilty conscience or a planned whitehat?"

## Multichain (II) — 2023-07-07 — $126M
*source slug: multichain-rekt (500) → resolved to multichain-rekt2*
- Chain/Attack vector: Multichain cross-chain bridge (Ethereum/Fantom/Moonriver); abnormal withdrawals from the MPC-controlled lockup addresses — direct control of the bridge's signing addresses, not a contract bug.
- Root cause: Undetermined at article time: transaction behavior suggested the attacker controlled the MPC addresses directly, with plausible vectors being a back-end breach, spearphished private keys, or a malicious insider. Precedent: the 2021 Anyswap (v3) hack back-calculated private keys from repeated transaction data.
- Mechanics: On July 6 the Multichain MPC lockup assets were moved to unknown addresses: $126.3M total, ~50% of the FTM bridge and ~80% of the Moonriver bridge — wBTC $30.9M, USDC $30.1M, wETH $13.4M, DAI/LINK/USDT/CRV $16.7M, Moonriver USDC/USDT/DAI/wBTC $7.5M, USDC $27.7M across six exploiter addresses. The team's public response: "not sure what happened… currently investigating," advising users to suspend use and revoke all Multichain approvals. Funds were not swapped or moved at the time of writing, and >$65M could be frozen by Tether/Circle.
- Lesson: Bridge security reduces to custody of the signing keys — audit the MPC/HSM opsec, key-ceremony design, and whether the team can even explain its control flows; vague "force majeure" comms without technical detail are a red flag. Past key-derivation flaws (Anyswap v3) and the 2022 approvals-drain should be mapped onto the current attack surface of any bridge.
- Aftermath: Second leaderboard entry after the $8M Anyswap hack in 2021; May 2023 had already brought bridging delays, insider-dump rumors and team-arrest reports papered over as "force majeure." Fantom, heavily reliant on Multichain versions of USDC/USDT/DAI/wETH/wBTC, was left without answers — rekt called it the largest rug they'd ever seen (or "a very shy whitehat").

## Nomad Bridge — 2022-08-02 — $190M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum bridge (ecosystem damage to Moonbeam, EVMOS, Milkomeda); broken message validation after a routine upgrade let anyone call `process()` and execute arbitrary messages — a permissionless, copy-paste crowd drain.
- Root cause: A June 2022 routine upgrade initialized the Replica contract with `0x00` as a trusted merkle root, so all messages validated by default. The check at line 185 of `process()` passed when the messages value was 0 — invalid under legacy logic, but 0x00 was defined as a trusted root in the upgrade — so every `process()` call executed as "proven" without any merkle proof.
- Mechanics: After one failed attempt (costing $350k in gas) the original attacker's tx called `process()` directly without proving validity; with 0x00 as trusted root the check passed. Because the tx was public, copycats simply copy-pasted the same calldata from Etherscan and swapped in their own address — a 2.5-hour free-for-all of crowdhacking, MEV bots and self-declared whitehats drained $190M. The top three addresses took $47M, $40M and $8M; the Rari Capital (Arbitrum) exploiter returned for ~$3M in stables, straight into Tornado. The team halted it by removing the Replica contract as owner — three hours after the exploit began.
- Lesson: Treat zero-value initialization of security-critical config (trusted roots, signers, thresholds, roles) as a never-trusted sentinel and assert on it in every upgrade's post-deploy checks. Public exploit calldata converts a 1-of-1 hack into a permissionless crowd drain — bridge pause/kill-switch procedures must be rehearsed to fire in minutes, and upgrades of message-validation logic need regression tests for "unproven" inputs.
- Aftermath: The 100th rekt leaderboard entry. Quantstamp's June audit had flagged a similar issue (QSP-19) with the remark "We believe the Nomad team has misunderstood the issue"; the team's acknowledgement came three hours late. Moonbeam TVL fell $300M→$135M, EVMOS ~$7M→$3M, Milkomeda $31M→$20M; 🍉🍉🍉.eth took $4M and claimed whitehat.

## OKX DEX — 2023-12-13 — $2.7M
*source slug: okx-rekt (500) → resolved to okx-dex-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; private-key compromise of the OKX DEX proxy's admin owner → malicious proxy upgrade → drain of every user approval still held by the TokenApprove contract.
- Root cause: The trusted DEX Proxy (0x55b35bf627944396f9950dd6bddadb5218110c76) is managed by a Proxy Admin (0x3c18F8554362c3F07Dc5476C3bBeB9Fdd6F6a500) whose owner key was compromised; the attacker upgraded the proxy to an implementation that directly calls `claimTokens` on the DEX contract (0x70cbb871e8f30fc8ce23609e9e0ea87b6b222f58) to transfer any tokens users had approved to TokenApprove (0x40aa958dd87fc8305b97f2ba922cddca374bcd7f).
- Mechanics: Users who traded on OKX DEX had approved the TokenApprove contract; the DEX contract's `claimTokens` allows a trusted DEX Proxy to pull those approved balances. Shortly before midnight UTC the attacker upgraded the proxy through the compromised Proxy Admin (upgrade txs 0xc6a5a7bc…, 0x22ebd2…) and began calling it to sweep approved tokens. SlowMist's initial estimate was ~$430k; PeckShield and Hacken traced the full $2.7M — 800 ETH (~$1.7M) and ~$620k in stablecoins across four more addresses. The suspected attacker was funded via Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: A proxy-admin key compromise turns every historical user approval into a live drain — when auditing an aggregator/DEX, map the full chain (TokenApprove → claimTokens → trusted proxy → proxy admin owner) and check admin-key custody, because the blast radius is the sum of all outstanding allowances. Users' approvals to deprecated contracts are permanent attack surface: protocols must actively force-revoke or kill retired proxies.
- Aftermath: OKX called the affected contract "a deprecated version, now secured" — but only after it got rekt, and initially promised just ~$370k in reimbursements. rekt's jab: even the security auditors writing up the incident copy-pasted a phishing address after mistaking an address-poisoning fake transfer for a real 300-ETH dispersal.

## PAID Network — 2021-03-05 — ~$3.1M realized ($27.4M nominal)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; unlimited mint of PAID tokens and dump into the Uniswap PAID/ETH pair. Suspected insider job — contract ownership was transferred to the attacker's address 30 minutes before the mint.
- Root cause: The PAID contract's mint was unprotected (no cap, no access control beyond the owner role), and the deployer EOA transferred contract ownership to the attacker 30 minutes before the mint — n2ckchong's on-chain finding, correlating with banteg's "one founder rugged another."
- Mechanics: The attacker minted 59,471,745.571 PAID just after 18:00 UTC and dumped them into the Uniswap PAID/ETH pool over the following ~30 minutes, swapping for 2,079.6 WETH (~$3.1M realized) and leaving 594,717,455.71 PAID (~$24.3M nominal) in the account — rekt's "Total Rugged Amount" $27.4M. PAID price fell from ~$2.86 to ~$0.32. WARONRUGS had publicly warned about the mint capability over a month earlier.
- Lesson: An uncapped, ownership-gated mint on a project token is the single highest-yield rug primitive — check who holds the mint/owner role, whether it changed hands near the incident, and the ownership-transfer timestamps. Public pre-incident warnings (WARONRUGS, a month before) are forensic gold: check the warning history before attributing anything to a sophisticated external hack.
- Aftermath: An on-chain note congratulated the "Russian Hackers" on "putin in the work"; CEO Kyle Chasse pleaded private-key compromise while PAID promised to restore all balances with a new contract. Rekt's framing: "an infinite mint is a magical thing — a golden ticket."

## PancakeBunny — 2021-05-20 — $45M
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC; 8-flash-loan price manipulation of PancakeSwap LP pool reserves feeding the `VaultFlipToFlip` reward calculation, minting 697k BUNNY and dumping it.
- Root cause: The protocol priced LP tokens using live spot reserves from PancakeSwap pools (BNB-BUSDT / BNB-BUNNY); the reward math was manipulable in a single transaction by skewing those reserves with flash loans, inflating the BUNNY reward claim.
- Mechanics: The attacker took 8 flash loans — 1.05M WBNB (WBNB+CAKE), 522.52K WBNB (WBNB+BUSD), 210.16K WBNB (WBNB+ETH), 133.5K WBNB (WBNB+BTCB), 241.02K WBNB (WBNB+SAFEMOON), 98.5K WBNB (WBNB+BELT), 66.29K WBNB (WBNB+DOT) and 2.96M USDT from Fortube. They deposited 2.96M USDT + 7,886 WBNB into WBNB+BUSDT (minting 144.45K LP), swapped 2.32M WBNB→3.83M BUSDT to skew the pool valuation, then called `getReward()` on VaultFlipToFlip to claim 6.97M BUNNY with the inflated LP valuation (dev team got a separate 1.05M BUNNY). Selling the BUNNY dropped its price from $146 to $6 — ~$45M lost; PancakeBunny's TVL fell from a $10B+ peak to ~$1B.
- Lesson: Reward/valuation logic must never read spot DEX reserves inside the same transaction context — require TWAP/oracle pricing for vault reward claims (hunt `getReward`/claim paths that multiply shares by current reserve ratios). Also: the audited code and the deployed code diverged — Haechi's audit predated the exploited contracts; treat post-audit upgrades as unaudited code.
- Aftermath: Haechi's statement: the exploited `helper` function was in new contracts they never audited, though their report had warned about unaudited changeable external contracts and flash-loan weakness. BSC had a brutal day: Venus Protocol and WARONRUGS imploded within hours.

## Pickle Finance — 2020-11-22 — $19.7M DAI
*source slug: pickle-rekt (500) → resolved to pickle-finance-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; evil-jar attack — a fake Pickle Jar exploited the missing whitelist in the Controller's `swapExactJarForJar` to swap the cDAI jar's funds out. Not an arb; the attacker was highly EVM-savvy.
- Root cause: ControllerV4's `swapExactJarForJar` did not check that the target jar was whitelisted, so the attacker deployed a malicious "jar" and swapped the real jar's assets into it. Pickle Jars are a Yearn yVault fork, and the flaw mirrored a similar bug found in Yearn code a month earlier.
- Mechanics: The hacker created a fake Pickle Jar and used `swapExactJarForJar` to move the cDAI jar's funds into his evil jar, stealing 19.7M DAI. The team's emergency `withdrawAll` failed because withdrawals required the Governance DAO's 12-hour timelock, and the single multisig member able to bypass it was asleep. Rekt organized a war room (Stake Capital, banteg, samczsun, emilianobonassi, the Pickle team) that had to whitehat-drain the remaining vulnerable jars while ~$50M sat exposed — fighting off copycats and 1000%+ APY redeposit FOMO, and avoiding front-runs on the rescue txs.
- Lesson: Cross-contract asset-movement functions (`swapXForX`, migrate/switch paths) must whitelist counterpart contracts; the fork's own upstream (Yearn) had the same bug a month prior, so check upstream patches when auditing any fork. Governance timelocks that block emergency response turn a contained hack into a multi-jar race — design a rehearsed pause/bailout path, not just a timelock.
- Aftermath: Audits from MixBytes (Oct 3) and Haechi (Oct 20) both predated ControllerV4 (Oct 23) — the vulnerable `swapExactJarForJar` was never audited. The incident became Cover Protocol's first big insurance claim, and rekt used it to argue audits are snapshots, not guarantees.

## Pike Finance — 2024-05-03 — >$1.9M (two attacks)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum, Arbitrum, Optimism; unprotected initializer + storage-layout break in the spoke contracts enabled attackers to seize ownership and `upgradeToAndCall` malicious implementations. Hit twice: April 26 and April 30, 2024.
- Root cause: The `initialize` function was not protected against re-execution. The attacker called `initialize()` to add their address to `_isActive`, bypassing access control; by consuming version numbers they blocked reusable initializers and re-ran initialization on every upgrade. After the first fix, a dependency added to the code shifted the storage layout and moved the `initialized` variable, reopening initialization — enabling the second attack.
- Mechanics: April 26 on Arbitrum: the attacker (0xAdaF1626aEC26A7937aE7d1Fa0664e6E0904C1d0) initialized the target contract (0x7856493B59cdb1685757A6DcCe12425F6a6666a0), took owner permissions and upgraded the spoke to a malicious implementation to siphon funds. Pike's post-exploit patch altered the storage layout (moving the initialized flag), so on April 30 a second attacker (0x19066f7431df29a0910d287c8822936bb7d89e23) repeated the same class of attack across Ethereum, Arbitrum and Optimism, draining over $1.6M in ARB, OP and ETH — caught by Chain Aegis — bringing the combined loss to over $1.9M.
- Lesson: Every upgradeable contract needs an initializer guard (initialized flag + versioning) that provably survives storage-layout changes — verify layout stability across upgrades and that re-initialization is impossible from any caller. Post-incident patches are new attack surface: "we fixed it" without killing the initializer (and without re-auditing) is how the second exploit happened.
- Aftermath: Pike had no public audits and no bug bounty (both "Coming soon") and still listed testnet contracts; the team offered the April 26 attacker a 20% bounty on-chain while $6.45M presale holders demanded answers. Quill Audits produced the postmortem; rekt: "Fool me once… fool me twice, shame on me."
