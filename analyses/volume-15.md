## Saddle Finance — 2021-01-20 — ~$276K taken from early LPs (launch arbitrage, not an exploit)
*source slug: saddle-rekt (500) → resolved to saddle-finance-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; no attacker flaw — the Curve-fork's pools were arbitraged to pieces within hours of launch, with thin initial liquidity and a StableSwap port the team demonstrably did not understand.
- Root cause: A heavily-funded Curve fork launched without launch-liquidity protection; early LPs' deposits were absorbed by arbitrageurs exploiting price misalignment in the new pools, while the team blamed "high slippage" and only made frontend slippage warnings more explicit.
- Mechanics: At least three major arbs took 7.9 BTC (~$275,735) from early LPs within 6 minutes on Jan 19 (4.01 BTC at 16:06, 0.79 BTC at 16:08, 3.11 BTC at 16:12 UTC). Users depositing in the first hour "will never get out as much as they put in". The Quantstamp audit had flagged 14 issues and explicitly recommended fixing them before production deployment — nobody read it.
- Lesson: (1) Fork audits: porting an AMM without understanding its math inherits the original's failure modes at higher speed — audit the port and the launch parameters (initial liquidity seeding, slippage caps), not just the license. (2) Launch-hour LPs are the softest target in DeFi; launch-liquidity protection (seeded pools, gradual ramps) and pre-launch audit remediation are risk controls, not paperwork.
- Aftermath: rekt's full polemic against funded copycats ("Raise $4.2m, copy the code from Curve, and get rekt"); Curve's Ben Hauser on the copied gas optimizations; veCRV holders retaliated by voting to cut CRV rewards to the tBTC pool (Saddle's founders are linked to Thesis/tBTC).

## Rari Capital — 2021-05-08 — $11.1M (2.9k ETH, ETH pool)
*source slug: rari-rekt (500) → resolved to rari-capital-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum (plus a BSC leg); the same wallet that hit Value DeFi hours earlier reused the "evil token in pool" pattern through integrated-protocol callbacks (Alpha Homora / Alpaca Finance).
- Root cause: The Rari ETH pool trusted receipts obtained through integrated protocols without validating the underlying token: a fake token pooled on SushiSwap unlocked a callback payload via Alpha Homora that let the attacker obtain ibETH in the Rari pool with no real ETH supplied.
- Mechanics: BSC leg: fake token + PancakeSwap pool → Alpaca Finance approve() payload call → vSafeWBNB via Codex farm → convert to WBNB → bridge to Ethereum via Anyswap, repeated 2×, stealing 5,346 BNB (~$3.8M) swapped to 1k ETH. ETH leg: fake token pooled on SushiSwap → Alpha Homora payload call → ibETH in the Rari ETH pool → convert to ETH: 2.9k ETH (~$11.1M) stolen, another 1.7k ETH at risk before team action. ~$15M total across the two attacks.
- Lesson: (1) Pool/aggregator trust boundaries: if a pool accepts any token through an integrated protocol's callback, attacker-crafted "receipts" (ibETH etc.) must not be redeemable against real assets — whitelist tokens across every integration. (2) Serial cross-chain attackers repeat one mechanism (the Evil Pickle Jar pattern) across chains within hours — check same-day prior art on sibling chains when triaging.
- Aftermath: The attacker left an on-chain taunt at the involved protocols, tried to cancel it, but set gas too low and the message stayed visible for 20 minutes (banteg/dudesahn credits). Rekt's framing: a "cross-chain killer" fuelling the Rari attack with Value DeFi's blood money.

## Punk Protocol — 2021-08-13 — $8.95M (~$5M returned)
*source slug: punk-rekt (500) → resolved to punkprotocol-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; missing `initializer` modifier → re-initialization of `forgeAddress` in the CompoundModel, then a legitimate-looking `withdrawToForge` drain.
- Root cause: `initialize()` in the CompoundModel code had no initializer modifier, so anyone could re-run it and pass their own malicious contract as the `forgeAddress` parameter via `delegateCall`; the `OnlyForge` modifier then treated the malicious contract as the trusted forge.
- Mechanics: The attacker called `initialize()` with their own contract as forgeAddress, then `withdrawToForge` sent the assets controlled by the CompoundModel to the attacker. Stolen USDC was swapped to ETH via 1inch and sent to Tornado in 100-ETH chunks. A frontrunning-bot owner noticed and whitehat-frontran the other two pools, returning ~$5M but keeping $1M as an after-the-fact bounty ("my frontrun was not perfect, as I did end up sending $3M USDC to the original hacker").
- Lesson: (1) Every proxy-style `initialize()` must be guarded by an initializer modifier — enumerate all initializable contracts (models/controllers included) and verify the initialize-once invariant on the deployed bytecode. (2) Whitehat frontrunning of an in-progress drain is a real incident-response tool, but bounty terms must be agreed before funds are held, not negotiated as a ransom.
- Aftermath: "Punk is dead" opener; the hack landed a day after Poly Network's $611M. Rekt's closing meditation on whitehat ethics — the "whitehat" kept 16% ($1M) and forced the deal on the team; "whitehats set their own wages, while security auditors take their salaries and the blame."

## bZx — 2021-11-06 — ~$55M
*source slug: bZx-rekt (500 live; Wayback snapshot recovered, canonical live slug /bzx-rekt)*
- Chain/Attack vector: Polygon + BSC (then Ethereum for cash-out); admin-key compromise — phishing of a developer's personal wallet that controlled the deployments (no contract exploit).
- Root cause: A single dev EOA held admin control over bZx's Polygon and BSC deployments; a phishing email with a malicious Word-document macro compromised his personal mnemonic. Admin authority lived in a personal hot wallet — single point of failure.
- Mechanics: The attacker used the compromised EOA to drain BZRX from the contracts, then updated the contract code to extract tokens from any wallet that had granted approvals to the affected contracts. Stolen BZRX was bridged to Ethereum and deposited as collateral to borrow other assets — avoiding the liquidity problem of dumping a large BZRX position. SlowMist's running total reached ~$55M; Binance-side USDT and Circle USDC were frozen.
- Lesson: (1) Protocol admin rights must never sit behind a personal EOA — enforce hardware-wallet/multisig (ideally timelocked governance) custody for all deployments, and map "who controls which chain deployment" in every audit. (2) Contract-compromise blast radius includes every historical approver: approval-harvesting via upgraded contract code is the second-stage kill; revoke-approval tooling and approval monitoring matter.
- Aftermath: bZx's leaderboard debut went straight into the top 10 despite three prior incidents (Feb 2020 flash-loan pair $298K + $645K, Sept 2020 $8M later returned). The team stressed "the code itself hadn't been compromised" and reached out to the hacker to "chat and reach an agreement"; rekt's verdict: single-EOA control is "unimaginable for any responsible project" at $55M scale. (Card distilled from the Wayback snapshot of /bZx-rekt; live canonical slug is /bzx-rekt.)

## Saddle Finance (II) — 2022-05-01 — $11M (+$3.8M whitehat-rescued)
*source slug: saddle-rekt (500) → resolved to saddle-finance-rekt2*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; flash-loan metapool price manipulation via a stale MetaSwapUtils library in the sUSDv2 metapool.
- Root cause: The metapool swap path still used an OLD MetaSwapUtils version that priced the LP token without `virtualPrice`; Saddle had published the fix in December (after Synapse's $8.2M near-miss on the same code in Nov 2021) but never wired it into the metapool swaps.
- Mechanics: A series of flash-loan-assisted sUSD/saddleUSD-V2 swaps manipulated the LP token's price, which could then be swapped back for more sUSD: main tx 3,375 ETH + second tx 557 ETH ≈ $11M from the sUSDv2 metapool; BlockSec whitehat-rescued 1,357 ETH (~$3.8M) via the same bug. Funds began moving through Tornado Cash; the majority remained in the exploiter's address at write time. nerve.fi on BSC was hit by the identical vector.
- Lesson: (1) A published fix that isn't deployed — or isn't wired into every call path — is worse than no fix: verify the exact library version used by each swap path against the repo HEAD, because "fixed in the current version" doesn't fix the deployed swap calculation. (2) Metapool LP pricing must use virtualPrice; any metapool that prices LP tokens at face value is flash-loan-manipulable.

## BonqDAO — 2023-02-03 — $120M notional (~$2M realized)
*source slug: bonqdao-rekt (500) → resolved to bonq-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Polygon; two-stage Tellor oracle manipulation — collateral inflated in tx 1, users' collateral liquidated in tx 2.
- Root cause: BonqDAO valued WALBT collateral using TellorFlex's instant submitted value; staking exactly 10 TRB (~$175, the takeAmount) let anyone submit an arbitrary WALBT price. The price-feed contracts (ConvertedPriceFeed, ChainlinkPriceFeed, TellorPriceFeed) were post-audit additions never covered by Omniscia's audit.
- Mechanics: Stage 1: stake 10 TRB, `submitValue()` sets WALBT price to 5e28 ("1 ALBT = 5 billion MATIC"), create a trove with 0.1 WALBT collateral and mint 100M BEUR. Stage 2: reset WALBT price to near-zero, liquidate other users' WALBT collateral → ~113–114M WALBT. Notional loss ~$120M at hack-time token prices, but low liquidity limited realized proceeds to ~$1.7M (ETH + DAI); stolen BEUR dumped on Polygon for ~$500K. Attacker funded via Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: (1) Instant/spot-submitted oracle values for collateral valuation are a full-drain primitive — require TWAP/median aggregation, staleness bounds, circuit breakers, and whitelisted reporters on any collateral feed. (2) Contracts added after an audit are unaudited code: in due diligence, diff "deployed since audit" (price-feed adapters are the classic offender) and re-scope.
- Aftermath: samczsun's summary — the attacker said "btw 1 ALBT = 5 billion MATIC now" and Bonq said "ok". Telegram admins posted "FUD and spam will not be tolerated" while the drain was visible on-chain; rekt's retort: "using instant price feeds for collateral valuation will not be tolerated." TVL collapsed $13M → ~$100K; ALBT dumped ~75% and AllianceBlock reissued the token from a pre-hack snapshot; BEUR depegged ~25%.

## Platypus Finance — 2023-02-16 — $8.5M
*source slug: platypus-rekt (500) → resolved to platypus-finance-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Avalanche; flash-loan logic error in the USP solvency check — collateral withdrawn while the borrowed stablecoin was kept.
- Root cause: `emergencyWithdraw()` checked only whether the position was *currently* solvent and neglected the effect of the borrowed USP, so the attacker could withdraw all supplied collateral while keeping the 41.7M borrowed USP. The flawed mechanism was the new USP stablecoin, live just 10 days.
- Mechanics: Flash-loan 44M USDC → deposit into Platypus → use the LP tokens as collateral → borrow 41.7M USP → `emergencyWithdraw()` the collateral (the solvency check passes because the position looks solvent at that instant) → repay the flash loan → swap the USP through Platypus pools, draining the liquidity of USDC/USDT/DAI/BUSD. USP depegged >50%; $1.5M of stolen USDT was blacklisted; the $8.5M sat in the attacker's contract — freezable stables and no Tornado route suggested an amateur.
- Lesson: (1) Collateral-withdrawal checks must account for the *entire* position including debt effects — any solvency check evaluated at current-time state before the withdrawal is flash-loan-exploitable ("currently solvent" is not "solvent after withdrawal"). (2) OPSEC is incident-response tooling: ZachXBT identified the exploiter within hours via a reused ENS, leading to the later arrest in France.
- Aftermath: The USP launch promised "an extra layer of protection from the volatility that other stablecoins may experience" — "a statement worthy of a Darwin award?" The team publicly appealed to the doxxed exploiter with a bounty offer.

## Poly Network (II) — 2023-07-03 — $4.4M
*source slug: poly-rekt (500) → resolved to poly-network-rekt2*
- Chain/Attack vector: Multi-chain bridge (57 assets across 10 blockchains); compromised 3-of-4 multisig validating forged deposit proofs — no contract bug.
- Root cause: Three of the four multisig signers validated forged deposit proofs, granting the attacker the ability to unlock funds on destination chains. The bridge's entire security model was a 4-key threshold, thinner than the 5-of-9 Ronin setup rekt had already mocked.
- Mechanics: Lock a small amount of tokens on one chain, then withdraw a larger amount on another chain via forged proofs, repeated across assets and chains. The notional value of minted assets reached ~$42B, but low liquidity on destination chains meant only ~$4.4M could be cashed out, with further assets remaining in the attacker's address. Main ETH address 0xe0Afadad1d93704761c8550F21A53DE3468Ba599; EthCrossChainManager 0x14413419452aaf089762a0c5e95ed2a13bbc488c.
- Lesson: (1) Bridge custody thresholds ARE the audit: 3-of-4 EOA signing on a cross-chain bridge is the whole security model — require distinct operators, hardware custody, per-transfer limits and continuous balance monitoring. (2) Distinguish notional minted value from realized loss — the $42B-minted vs $4.4M-cashed gap is the bridge-liquidity reality that also caps attacker payout; report both numbers.
- Aftermath: "Poly Network is back… It seems we only ever hear of Poly Network when they're hacked." After the 2021 $611M hack ended with the whitehat returning funds and being offered a CSO role + $500k bounty, rekt asks: "Will Poly learn their lesson? Or will they go for the hat trick?"

## RocketSwap — 2023-08-15 — ~$869K
*source slug: rocket-rekt (500) → resolved to rocketswap-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Base; private keys brute-forced from the project's server — a web2 custody failure, then drained via the farm's proxy permissions.
- Root cause: The team stored signing private keys on the server to enable "offline signatures" for launchpad deployment; the server was brute-forced, and the farm's proxy contract concentrated multiple high-risk permissions behind those keys.
- Mechanics: At ~23:00 UTC on Aug 14 the farming contracts were drained via an internal address. The attacker bridged 472 ETH back to Ethereum and launched their own memecoin with 400 ETH of liquidity, hoping to ride the BALD-frenzy wave. The team shut down the farm, promised to redeploy without a proxy and relinquish minting privileges.
- Lesson: (1) Keys on servers are brute-forceable secrets — deployment keys must never persist server-side; use ephemeral signers or hardware custody, and treat "offline signature" excuses as the red flag they are. (2) A farm proxy with multiple high-risk permissions behind one key is a single-drain primitive — map proxy-admin power before launch and require multisig on asset-moving permissions.
- Aftermath: RocketSwap was the third Base casualty in two weeks after the BALD rugpull and LeetSwap; comments disabled on tweets and Telegram paused — rekt: "one wonders why they are so keen to control the narrative." SwirlLend rugged soon after, framing rekt's broader take on Base's "early days of BSC" phase.

## Platypus Finance (II) — 2023-10-13 — $2.2M
*source slug: platypus2-rekt (500) → resolved to platypus-rekt2*
- Chain/Attack vector: Avalanche; flash-loan slippage manipulation of the LP-AVAX pool via its internal `cash`/`liability` state.
- Root cause: Per Inspex, the attacker deposited to raise both LP contracts' liability, swapped sAVAX→WAVAX to reduce the LP-AVAX contract's cash, then withdrew to remove all available cash — inflating `slippageFrom` and manipulating `actualToAmount` so swaps settled at attacker-favorable prices.
- Mechanics: Three transactions, each flash-loan-manipulating pool prices and taking profit from the manipulated slippage. Like February, the hacker made mistakes that enabled partial recovery: $575K was rescued, leaving ~$1.6M of WAVAX/sAVAX in the primary attack contract. The Platypus fork Hummus Exchange pre-emptively paused its contracts. This was the third Platypus exploit in 8 months (Feb $8.5M, July $150K, Oct $2.2M).
- Lesson: (1) Slippage computed from manipulable internal pool state (`cash`/`liability`) is a flash-loan target — bounds derived from swap state must be clamped by external oracles or pool invariants, and the "withdraw removes all cash" sequence should be an explicit audit case. (2) Repeatedly-exploited protocols are upstream warnings for their forks: Hummus paused on the same day — monitor exploits of the code you forked.
- Aftermath: "After three exploits in 8 months… how long until Platypus goes extinct?" The Feb hacker's arrest in France is revisited; Hacken and Omniscia audits didn't cover the flaw.

## Radiant Capital — 2024-01-03 — 1,900 ETH (~$4.5M)
*source slug: radiant-rekt (500) → resolved to radiant-capital-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Arbitrum (also deployed on BSC); known Aave-V2-fork empty-market rounding bug exploited on the newly launched native USDC market.
- Root cause: In forked Aave V2 code, freshly launched (and therefore empty) markets have a window where a flash loan can manipulate collateral valuation via the combination of a rounding error and `totalSupply == 0`; upstream Aave mitigates by seeding an initial deposit on market creation — Radiant's fork didn't.
- Mechanics: The attack contract was deployed just six seconds after the new USDC market was activated; the attacker had clearly staged everything while waiting for the December 25th proposal to be enacted, and drained 1,900 ETH (~$4.5M). The team sent an on-chain message to the hacker and said they believe they're dealing with a whitehat "for various reasons".
- Lesson: (1) Fork operators must track upstream mitigations as patch deltas — Aave's "seed new markets with an initial deposit" is a hard requirement for forks; every upstream fix that the fork hasn't applied is an exploit waiting for its trigger event. (2) New-market launches are monitorable attack events: pre-staged contracts firing seconds after activation is the signature to alert on.
- Aftermath: "No current funds are at risk." — "So, just the $4.5M that had already been stolen, then?" Four audits (OpenZeppelin, BlockSec, Peckshield, Zokyo) didn't prevent it; rekt's running point: forks get free early-warning from the original project's larger TVL, but only if lessons are learned.

## PrismaFi — 2024-03-28 — $11.6M (3,258 wstETH)
*source slug: prism-rekt (500) → resolved to prismafi-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; Liquity-fork — flash-loan input-validation bug in the MigrateTroveZap migration helper (rekt's first restaking-platform exploit).
- Root cause: `MigrateTroveZap.onFlashloan()` lacked input validation, letting the attacker trigger `closeTrove`/`openTrove` on *arbitrary addresses* — including victims' troves — so a closed victim trove's collateral was refunded to the zap contract itself. The zap was a recent migration addition not covered by the MixBytes/Nomoi/Zellic audits.
- Mechanics: In the exploit tx the attacker closed a specific victim's trove, refunding 1,745 wstETH to the zap contract; opened a new trove spending 463 wstETH; ~1,282 wstETH then remained in the zap. The attacker opened their own trove, migrated it through the zap to consume the leftover 1,282 wstETH, closed it and extracted the profit — $11.6M over several txs. The protocol was paused by the emergency multisig ~4h later; mkUSD/ULTRA were claimed overcollateralized.
- Lesson: (1) Migration/zap helpers must validate that the troves being closed/opened belong to the caller — arbitrary close/open parameters make the helper a "refund-to-me" primitive; every callback invoked with user-supplied trove IDs is a hunt target. (2) Post-audit additions are unaudited code: diff deployed bytecode against the audited commit, especially for newly shipped migration/zap paths.
- Aftermath: One receiving address left the message "this is a white hat rescue" and offered a refund contact; Decurity spotted a deployed-but-unused copycat exploit; Liquity claims the bug is not replicable on its codebase. "The 'end game' can be gamified" — first known attack on a restaking platform, possibly opening Pandora's box.

## Radiant Capital (II) — 2024-10-17 — $53M+
*source slug: radiant-rekt (500) → resolved to radiant-capital-rekt2*
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC + Arbitrum (Ethereum and Base contracts pre-staged but unused); 3-of-11 multisig compromise → ownership transfer and malicious implementation upgrade.
- Root cause: The multisig required only 3 of 11 signatures and the attacker gained control of at least 3 signers (private-key compromise). The three-step kill: transfer ownership of the Pool Provider to a malicious contract, upgrade the lending-pool implementation, drain the pools.
- Mechanics: The malicious implementation was deployed 14 days before execution on several chains; a failed attempt on Arbitrum came six days prior. Attack txs on BSC (0xd97b93f6…) and ARB (0x7856552d…); users were told to revoke approvals to the listed contracts. Ancilia spotted $16M already gone on BSC before Radiant acknowledged anything; funds were swapped via 1inch/ParaSwap/PancakeSwap/Odos into ETH/BNB. Markets on Base and Mainnet were paused; identical malicious contracts sat dormant on Ethereum and Base.
- Lesson: (1) A 3-of-11 threshold means any 3 signer devices are the attack surface — ownership-transfer multisigs need hardware wallets, higher thresholds, and timelocks; count distinct operators, not signatures. (2) Pre-staged malicious implementations on multiple chains, weeks ahead of execution, are a premeditation signature — monitor proxy-admin changes and undeployed lookalike contracts across all chains a protocol operates on.
- Aftermath: "Radiant's 'robust' 3-of-11 multisig turned out to be as secure as a paper lock on a bank vault… the attacker didn't just find a chink in the armor; they waltzed through the front door with VIP access." Second major incident of 2024 after the Jan flash-loan; "lightning striking twice, or just shoddy electrical wiring?"

## Aztec Connect — 2026-06-14 — ~$2.28M (+~$88K second wave)
*source slug: aztec-network-rekt (500) → resolved to aztec-connect-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum L1 settlement of the deprecated Aztec Connect rollup; ZK proof/settlement boundary mismatch ("settlement boundary bypass" per SlowMist) — the proof didn't bind the fields execution used.
- Root cause: `computeRootHashes()` only validated the beginning of the submitted `_proofData`, while the parameters controlling actual token transfers sat in the middle, unchecked and functionally invisible to the proof; `numRealTxs` was not effectively bound to the transaction set enforced by the ZK proof. The contract was immutable — Aztec Labs deprecated Aztec Connect in 2023 and renounced admin keys in April 2024, leaving $2.28M of unclaimed user funds inside.
- Mechanics: At 12:26 UTC the attacker batched 14 valid `processRollup` calls into RollupProcessorV3 in one atomic tx, executing public withdrawals: 909 ETH, 270,513 DAI, 167.89 wstETH, plus LUSD, yvDAI, yvWETH, yvLUSD (~$2.28M). Blockaid flagged a pre-exploit contract deployment ~6 minutes before the drain; CertiK went public 86 minutes after, Aztec Labs 74 minutes after CertiK with a four-sentence statement. A second attacker (fresh wallet funded via a relayer) submitted 14 more rollup calls on Jun 14–15, sweeping the residual DeFi bridge positions for another ~$88K.
- Lesson: (1) The ZK proof must bind EVERY field the settlement layer reads — a mismatch between what the verifier checks and what execution consumes (calldata-boundary/offset gaps) is the rollup equivalent of arbitrary execution; fuzz the proof-data boundary and assert numTxs ≤ proven set size. (2) Deprecated-but-funded immutable contracts are permanent attack surface: deprecation plans must include forced-exit paths, remaining-balance sweeps, and continued monitoring — "the hope that nobody reads its open-source code" is not a defense.
- Aftermath: The four-entity distinction (Aztec Labs / Aztec Connect / Aztec Foundation / current Aztec Network) and the foundation's ring-fencing of the AZTEC token; contrast with Syscoin's same-week detailed post-mortem; a $450K bounty had been paid in 2023 for a different critical bug on the same codebase — "admin keys made that fix possible. By the time this flaw surfaced, those keys had been gone for over two years."

## Aztec Bridge (Aztec 2.0 escape hatch) — 2026-06-17 — ~$2.197M
*source slug: aztec-network-rekt (500) → resolved to aztec-bridge-rekt*
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; ZK-circuit binding gap in the deprecated Aztec 2.0 rollup's escape hatch — the same "unbound root" class as the Aztec Connect drain, different contract, different entry point.
- Root cause: The escape-hatch circuit used `old_data_root` in two places: fed into the join-split circuit for note-membership checks, and exposed as a public input compared in Solidity against the live on-chain root — with NO equality constraint between the two. The attacker proved membership against a fake Merkle tree of fabricated notes and supplied the real on-chain root as the public input. The vulnerable circuit had been removed from the codebase in PR #402, but the deployed verifier still held the old verification key.
- Mechanics: At 18:21 UTC a fresh wallet funded with 0.134 ETH from HitBTC; 13 minutes later `escapeHatch()` released 1,158 ETH (~$2.04M) in a single call (18:34), then 150,000 DAI (18:49) and 0.4696 renBTC (18:50) — three txs in ~37 minutes, ~$2.197M total. The proof header carried rollupSize/numTxs = 0 while real withdrawal parameters sat deeper in the data: verification read the zeroes, execution (hardcoded to process ≥1 tx at line 512) read the withdrawals. The attacker's proof stated deposit = 0, withdraw = 1,158 ETH with fabricated source notes — impossible in a legitimate proof.
- Lesson: (1) When one circuit value plays multiple roles (membership root AND public-input root), every use must be equality-constrained — "two independent witnesses with no equality constraint" is the same proof-vs-execution binding failure as the Connect bug, exploitable through a different door. (2) Removing a circuit from the repo doesn't remove it from the chain: deployed verification keys are immutable code — track key lifecycle and disable legacy verifiers/escape hatches at deprecation time.
- Aftermath: Three "clean exits" in 37 minutes with surgical on-chain traces (0.00009 ETH gas, rollupId 4487); Aztec Labs separated the incident from the Jun 14 drain while omitting the mechanism — the full account came from BlockSec Phalcon and the incident report; rekt's framing question: "When the code says one thing and the deployed verifier still accepts another, which one is the contract actually running?"
