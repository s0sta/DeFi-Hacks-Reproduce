## ChainSwap — July 13, 2021 — $4.4M (second hack of the month; first was $800k)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + BSC — cross-chain bridge; unauthorized minting via per-token proxy Factory contracts (ETH side), then cashing out via 1inch; token-draining swaps on PancakeSwap (BSC side).
- Root cause: Broken authorization on the bridge's per-token Factory (minting) contracts: the "sloppy auth check" accepted a fresh address as signature on every transaction, so anyone who paid the 0.005 ETH chargeFee could mint any bridged token to an arbitrary `to` address.
- Mechanics: The attacker called the receive/mint function of each token's Factory contract, used a new address as the auth signature each tx, paid 0.005 ETH, set `to` to a chosen address and repeated — the attacker's wallet shows a long series of 0.005 ETH dust transactions, each one minting fresh tokens. On BSC, 20 tokens were hit; e.g. WilderWorld saw 40 txs × 500,000 WILD = 20M WILD, sold for ~650 WBNB (~$200k) via PancakeSwap, depleting the WILD/WBNB pool. Total ~$4.4M was accumulated, with 456 ETH (~$935k) bridged back and sold via 1inch in 5 transactions.
- Lesson: Bridge mint functions must not accept caller-supplied "signatures"/addresses as auth — enumerate every mint path of a bridge and check it is gated by real signatures or a fixed minter set. Cross-chain bridges are both the target and the exit route: track dust-fee mint calls and cross-chain sell-side swaps when hunting.
- Aftermath: The attacker's wallet was funded from Tornado Cash yet converted a bounty into freezable USDT, leading rekt to speculate about an insider or false-flag; ChainSwap arranged a full compensation plan but kept its tarnished reputation. Exploit breakdown credited to @cmichelio.

## CoinDCX — July 18–19, 2025 — $44.3M
- Chain/Attack vector: Centralized exchange (hot-wallet drain) — ETH + Solana; no smart-contract flaw, a server-side compromise of exchange infrastructure.
- Root cause: Undisclosed server breach of CoinDCX's signing/infrastructure; the drained wallet was not publicly tagged nor included in the exchange's proof-of-reserves, so the loss required manual attribution via counterparty analysis. (Not a contract bug.)
- Mechanics: July 16 — 1 ETH left Tornado Cash, passed through FixedFloat, Polygon, and deBridge to Solana. July 18 21:07 UTC — a single USDT test transaction; 22:09–22:14 UTC — a five-minute systematic drain of $2M, $7M, $10M, $10M, $5M, $5M and a final $5M (≈$28.3M SOL + $15.78M ETH), split across two Solana addresses, then bridged to Ethereum (0xEF0c5b9E…D2 holding ~$44.3M). CoinDCX stayed silent for ~17 hours and only disclosed at 14:48 UTC on July 19 — seven minutes after ZachXBT's public Telegram post at 14:41 UTC.
- Lesson: CEX "all funds safe / no customer funds impacted" claims are falsifiable on-chain — hunt untagged exchange hot wallets through counterparty and flow clustering, and treat Tornado→FixedFloat→deBridge pre-staging as a laundering signature. Multi-day rehearsal (test tx → timed batch drains) is a professional-actor signature worth logging for every CEX incident.
- Aftermath: ZachXBT forced the disclosure (mirroring WazirX's $235M silence in July 2024); some sources linked the prep to Lazarus Group; CoinDCX offered up to a 25% recovery bounty while claiming $538M in reserves absorbed the hit.

## Compound — Sep 29 – Oct 4, 2021 — ~$147M COMP ("bank error", $80M first wave + $68.8M second)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — buggy COMP distribution in the updated Comptroller (Proposal 62), then continued by anyone calling the permissionless `drip()` on the Reservoir vault.
- Root cause: Faulty COMP distribution logic in the newly deployed Comptroller contract that sent excess COMP to borrowers, plus a second flaw: `drip()` on the Reservoir (which accumulates 0.5 COMP per block) was callable by anyone, refilling the vulnerable Comptroller so the "bank error" could be repeated before fix Proposal 64 passed.
- Mechanics: After the Sep 29 Comptroller update, ~$80M of excess COMP was wrongly distributed. The Reservoir had not been drained for ~2 months and held 200k+ COMP (~$68M); any user could call `drip()` to refill the Comptroller, and 3.5 days later the Reservoir was drained and another $68.8M flowed into the vulnerable vault. Banteg's tally: $80M initial + $22M already claimed + $45M at risk = $147M total.
- Lesson: For every rewards/refill mechanism, ask "who can trigger accrual, and what happens if the distribution logic is buggy?" — permissionless drip/accrue functions turn a one-time bug into a repeatable drain. When a protocol bug is found, model the full vault lifecycle (reserves already accrued) rather than only the first loss.
- Aftermath: Founder Robert Leshner threatened doxxed recipients with the IRS ("return 90%, keep 10% as white-hat"), a threat that damaged Compound's reputation more than the loss; contrasted with Alchemix's polite appeal, which recovered 55% of its $6.5M mistaken payout.

## Cream Finance (1st) — August 30, 2021 — $18.8M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — Cream lending market; flash-loan collateralized borrow plus reentrancy through the AMP token's ERC77/ERC1820 pre-transfer hook.
- Root cause: The AMP token implements ERC77-style ERC1820 `_callPreTransferHooks`, which enables reentrancy during transfer; Cream's `borrow()` lacked a reentrancy guard, so a nested borrow executed before the first borrow's state update. The bug arrived with the Feb 10, 2021 proposal adding the AMP market — after the Trail of Bits audit.
- Mechanics: Contract A flash-loaned 500 WETH as collateral to mint 24.17k crETH, borrowed 19.48M AMP, then reentered `borrow()` inside the AMP transfer to take another 355 ETH before the position was updated. Contract B, funded with half (9.74M) of A's AMP, liquidated part of A's loan to redeem 187 WETH back to A; A repaid the flash loan with the reentrancy ETH and kept a profit of 41 ETH + 9.74M AMP per cycle. The process was repeated across 17 transactions, netting ~6k ETH (~$18.8M) plus 418.3M AMP tokens.
- Lesson: When a lending protocol lists a NEW collateral token, the token contract itself is part of the attack surface — audit ERC777/ERC77-style pre/post transfer hooks for reentrancy before listing. Always require `nonReentrant` on borrow/lend (as @muditgupta noted, that alone would have stopped it).
- Aftermath: Cream's first direct attack (earlier involvement was the Alpha Finance incident); stolen ETH remained parked at 0xce1f4b4f…6ede at time of writing; analysis credited to PeckShield.

## BurgerSwap — May 28, 2021 — $7.2M
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC — flash-swap + attacker-created fake token pair + reentrancy in the AMM's swap before reserves update.
- Root cause: BurgerSwap's fork of the UniV2 pair contract had the `x*y=k` invariant check removed, and allowed reentrancy — a second swap executed before reserves (used for output calculation) were updated. With the k-check gone, any output size could be swapped for one unit of input.
- Mechanics: Flash-swap 6k WBNB from PancakeSwap, swap most to 92k BURGER on BurgerSwap, then create a pair of 100 fake (non-standard BEP-20) tokens + 45k BURGER. Swap the 100 fake tokens for 4.4k WBNB through the new pair, then reenter and swap the 45k BURGER for another 4.4k WBNB before reserves updated — 8.8k WBNB total — swap back 493 WBNB to 108.7k BURGER and repay the flash swap. Across ~14 "servings": 4.4k WBNB ($1.6M), 1.4M USDT ($1.4M), 432k BURGER ($3.2M), 142k xBURGER ($1M), 95k ROCKS and more.
- Lesson: When auditing a forked AMM, diff it against the original — a deleted invariant check (`x*y>=k`) or missing reentrancy guard is the highest-value finding. Also check that pairs accept non-standard tokens whose accounting can be manipulated during the same block.
- Aftermath: Fourth BSC copycat hack of the week (~3am UTC+8), broken down publicly by @FrankResearcher; BurgerSwap promised a detailed compensation plan; rekt's piece repeatedly asks whether the missing check was negligence or something "seedier".

## Conic Finance — July 21, 2023 — $4.2M (two attacks: $3.3M ETH omnipool + ~$934k crvUSD omnipool)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — (1st) read-only reentrancy price manipulation via CurveLPOracleV2; (2nd) sandwich-style exchange against loosely bounded imbalanced Curve pools.
- Root cause (1st): Read-only reentrancy in the new oracle contract CurveLPOracleV2 let the attacker reenter `rETH-f.totalSupply()` (and steCRV etc.) to skew prices and withdraw more than deposited. Peckshield had flagged the same issue in the original oracle audit, but the newly introduced V2 oracle was outside audit scope. Conic's own reentrancy guard never triggered because it assumed ETH = address 0xeee… while Curve uses the WETH address for V2 pools.
- Root cause (2nd): Conic's bounds for interacting with imbalanced Curve pools were too loose, so the attacker could drain the crvUSD pool slowly via repeated deposit/withdraw loops around favorable exchanges.
- Mechanics (1st): Deposit into the ETH omnipool → reenter the oracle's view functions mid-withdrawal to manipulate prices → withdraw more than deposited; over 1,700 ETH (~$3.3M) was forwarded to 0x3d32c5a2…3010. (2nd): exchange crvUSD→USDC in the Curve pool, deposit crvUSD into Conic, exchange USDC→crvUSD at the now-favorable rate, withdraw from Conic, repeat — ~$934k stolen, ~$300k profit. Both attacks were frontrun by MEV bots; the second bot returned 81 ETH (90% of its profit) the next day.
- Lesson: Read-only reentrancy in price oracles is now a standard vault kill-shot — audit every oracle/view contract the vault reads, including NEW versions deployed after the last audit, and verify a reentrancy guard actually triggers for the addresses the pool really uses (ETH vs WETH assumptions silently break guards). For omnipools: tighten imbalance bounds so single-pool price skew can't be converted into a withdraw surplus.
- Aftermath: Conic was one of the most anticipated Curve-Wars projects (touted as the next CVX/Yearn); CNC fell from ~$6 to $1.72 and TVL to under a third of its pre-hack level; the first frontrunner address had been labeled "LadyPepe Token Exploiter" by BlockSec.

## Cover Protocol — December 28, 2020 — $9.4M taken, $3.2M recovered (~$6.2M net); COVER supply inflated 48 quadrillion %
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — infinite mint via the Blacksmith shield-mining reward accounting (storage/memory bug) after a new Balancer pool was added.
- Root cause: Reward-calculation bug in the Blacksmith farming contract: claiming with a ~1 wei BPT balance (when one user held all of a pool's liquidity) hit a storage/memory issue that minted 40,796,131,214,802,600,000 COVER (supply up from 84,477).
- Mechanics: Dec 28 04:09 UTC, the team multisig added a new Balancer pool to Blacksmith for the new coverage expirations. At 08:08 an attacker deposited 1,326,880 BPT and withdrew 1,326,878.99 BPT while claiming ~703.64 COVER. Grap Finance repeated the trick at the extreme: deposit 15,255.55 BPT, withdraw all but 1 wei, wait for the last other user to withdraw, redeposit, then claim — the reward math on the 1-wei balance minted the 40.8-quintillion COVER. Grap sold via 1inch for 4,350 ETH (~$4.4M) and returned it to the team as a "white hat"; six addresses total exploited the loophole before it was closed.
- Lesson: Reward-claim arithmetic must be fuzz-tested at extreme balances — a single user holding ~all pool liquidity (1 wei dust) is the classic trigger for minting bugs. When a pool/reward contract is added by multisig, re-audit the reward rebasing it introduces.
- Aftermath: COVER fell 40x in 4 hours while Grap's GRAP token pumped 40x (24h volume $236 → $5.46M); the protocol's fourth token iteration (SAFE → SAFE2 → COVER → new snapshot token) was planned to refund users; rekt floated the "insider job gone wrong" theory after MXC listed GRAP the same day.

## Cream Finance (2nd) — October 27, 2021 — ~$130M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — flash-loan leverage loop across two addresses, then manipulation of yUSDVault's `pricePerShare` to double collateral value and drain the lending vaults.
- Root cause: CREAM's PriceOracleProxy priced yUSDVault via `pricePerShare = yUSD balance / totalSupply(yUSDVault)` — a live, manipulable ratio — and allowed yUSDVault shares as collateral, so redeem-and-redeposit could double the reported value of the attacker's collateral.
- Mechanics: Address A flash-loaned 500M DAI from MakerDAO → Curve yPool yDAI → mint yUSD → deposit into Yearn's yUSD strategy, then used the yUSDVault shares as CREAM collateral to mint ~$500M crYUSD. Address B flash-loaned $2B ETH from AAVE as collateral to borrow ~$500M yUSD, deposited it for more crYUSD; A and B looped deposit/borrow with B passing ~$500M yUSDVault to A each round until A held ~$1.5B crYUSD + $500M yUSDVault. Redeeming ~$500M yUSDVault shrank the vault's totalSupply to $8M; depositing ~$8M yUSD doubled the share price, so CREAM valued A's collateral at $3B — $2B of ETH repaid B's AAVE loan, ~$500M yUSD repaid A's DAI loan, and the remaining ~$1B of crYUSD borrowing capacity drained CREAM's ~$130M lending assets.
- Lesson: Never price a yield-bearing vault share as live balance ÷ totalSupply — deposit/redeem is a two-way manipulation primitive that can double or halve the ratio in one block; use immutable or time-averaged oracle prices for LP/vault-share collateral, or disallow them entirely in lending markets.
- Aftermath: The exploit tx input data carried the message "gÃTµ Baave lucky, iron bank lucky, cream not. ydev : incest bad, dont do" — Mudit Gupta read it as experienced DeFi devs with a grudge in the Yearn ecosystem; Cream's deployer begged "you win. we're rekt… 10% bounty"; the attacker offloaded via renBridge and $40M+ of single-sided CRETH2 liquidity.

## Crypto.com — January 17, 2022 — $33.7M (~4.8k ETH ≈ $15M + 444 BTC ≈ $18.7M)
- Chain/Attack vector: Centralized exchange — user-wallet drain bypassing 2FA and the withdrawal whitelist (ETH side) plus a large BTC hot-wallet sweep; no contract flaw.
- Root cause: Never disclosed; the bypass of 2FA, email withdrawal approvals and the withdrawal whitelist implies a compromise of exchange API/infrastructure (server-side), not a smart-contract bug.
- Mechanics: Jan 17 04:44 UTC Crypto.com tweeted "All funds are safe"; CertiK counted ~4,836 ETH drained from ~282 user wallets into Tornado Cash. ErgoBTC's follow-up found the payout wallet bc1q7cyrf…pemf making an abnormally large withdrawal that was consolidated into 67.75 BTC outputs, then serialized 24–25 BTC deposits into a BTC tumbler used by Lazarus Group and Darkside ransomware; 173 BTC sat unmixed at bc1qk8wlw…jsq. Together the two legs total ~$33.7M, and Crypto.com never acknowledged a loss at the time.
- Lesson: CEX hot/payout wallets can be clustered and monitored on-chain, so "all funds safe" claims are testable — track tumbler deposit patterns (24–25 BTC chunks) as a laundering fingerprint. 2FA-bypassing wallet drains are an infrastructure/API compromise signature, distinct from contract exploits; don't file them as smart-contract findings.
- Aftermath: CEO Kris Marszalek kept posting "all funds safe" while users reported losses; rekt's article was updated after ErgoBTC's investigation doubled the figure; a Deloitte SOC2 audit didn't stop it — the exchange quietly refunded affected users later.

## Curve Finance (frontend) — August 9, 2022 — ~$575k (340 ETH)
- Chain/Attack vector: Web2 DNS hijack of curve.fi — mirrored malicious frontend harvesting token approvals; on-chain leg drained via FixedFloat, Binance and Tornado Cash.
- Root cause: Compromise of the DNS nameservers at registrar iwantmyname (not a Curve account hack, per Michael Egorov: "their ns compromised… switched the ns"), letting the attacker point curve.fi at a clone site that prompted users to approve a malicious contract (0x9eb5f8e8…8881).
- Mechanics: Users who approved on the fake UI had ~340 ETH (~$575k) taken by 0x50f9202e…f331. The attacker deposited 292 ETH to FixedFloat (112 ETH frozen), 20 ETH to Binance, and 27.7 ETH to Tornado Cash. curve.exchange (the alternate UI) was unaffected and the team directed users there while the mirrored site was taken down, though some nameservers stayed stale.
- Lesson: Treat the frontend as part of the target: audit DNS/registrar configs, nameserver change controls, and whether the dapp is served via IPFS/ENS. Approval-harvesting via hijacked UIs converts years of smart-contract hardening into a $575k siphon, so check for malicious approval contracts and stale nameservers in any Web3 recon.

## dYdX v3 — November 17, 2023 — $9M from the v3 insurance fund
- Chain/Attack vector: Perpetuals market manipulation — one actor built outsized YFI long open interest on dYdX, then crashed YFI spot to force underwater liquidations covered by the insurance fund.
- Root cause: Loose risk parameters for low-liquidity markets: YFI open interest on dYdX spiked from $0.8M to $67M driven by a single on-chain-traceable actor (higher than any other perp venue), and the v3 insurance fund was the backstop that absorbed the liquidation gaps when YFI spot dumped ~40% after a 2x weekly pump.
- Mechanics: The actor previously attempted the same play on the SUSHI market two weeks earlier. After the YFI spot crash (~$200M market cap wiped), dYdX's liquidations on the YFI market were filled with $9M from the insurance fund (leaving $13.5M); no user funds were affected. dYdX added extra margin requirements for lower-liquidity tokens in response.
- Lesson: Watch open-interest concentration in low-liquidity perp markets — one actor holding most of a market's OI is a manipulation red flag, and the liquidation insurance fund is the real payout target. For perp venues, the hunting checklist is: per-market margin requirements, OI caps, and whether insurance-fund accounting can be forced into covering a coordinated spot dump.
- Aftermath: Founder Antonio Juliano characterized it as an intentional, well-capitalized market manipulation targeting the insurance pool; the team refused to negotiate with the exploiter and offered bounties for identifying them — critics flagged the unilateral risk-parameter change as skirting decentralized decision-making.
