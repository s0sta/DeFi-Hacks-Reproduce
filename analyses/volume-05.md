# Analyses — Volume 05

## Harvest Finance — Oct 26, 2020 — ~$33.8M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; flash-loan-driven price manipulation of a Curve pool feeding a yield-vault deposit/withdraw oracle.
- Root cause: The FARM_USDT/USDC vault priced LP shares with an internal price index derived from the underlying Curve pool, but the arbitrage check's tolerance was set too low while the default 3% slippage tolerance was too high — so depositing at an artificially pumped USDT price and withdrawing at the dumped price netted more USDT than deposited.
- Mechanics: With a $50M USDT flash loan the attacker repeatedly swapped 11.4M USDC→USDT on Curve Y to push USDT up, deposited 60.6M USDT into the fUSDT vault, swapped back to depress the price, then withdrew 61.1M USDT (~$0.5M profit per loop) because shares redeemed against the manipulated index. ~4 loops fit per 10M-gas block; the attacker ran 32 loops in ~7 minutes, then converted to renBTC and exited via Tornado Cash. Drains stopped at $25M of an available $400M; the pie split roughly $24M hacker / $6M Uniswap LPs / $2.5M Harvest devs / $1M Curve LPs.
- Lesson: Any vault that prices deposits/withdrawals off a spot AMM it itself touches is flash-loan-arbitrageable — check every "price index" path for swap-induced pump/dump asymmetry. Tighten slippage AND raise the arbitrage/deviation tolerance on share accounting, not just one.
- Aftermath: REKT framed it as a "skilled farmer", and even published a whistleblower note suggesting the 3% slippage looked planted. Harvest (audited by PeckShield/Haechi/CertiK 3 weeks earlier) called it an "arbitrage economic attack"; attacker refunded ~$2.48M to the deployer, redistributed pro-rata.

## HECO Bridge / HTX — Nov 22, 2023 — ~$99M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum (HECO Chain's ETH bridge) + HTX hot wallets; compromised operator/hot-wallet private keys.
- Root cause: Private-key compromise of the HECO bridge operator account (no smart-contract flaw found); likely reused credentials/foothold from the earlier HTX and Poloniex breaches, given the entities' shared infrastructure.
- Mechanics: From 09:59 UTC the compromised operator (0x3d655889…) withdrew bridge funds (0xa929022c…) to the attacker 0xfc146d1c…, forwarding to an accumulation wallet: 42M USDT, >10k ETH ($19M), 489 HBTC ($18.8M), 347M SHIB ($2.8M), plus UNI/USDC/LINK/TUSD → $86.6M per PeckShield. At 10:41 UTC two compromised HTX hot wallets sent 1,240 ETH, 7.3M USDT, 1.78M USDC, 62.2K LINK ($12.5M) to two attacker addresses. Total ≈$99M, 12 days after Poloniex lost $126M and weeks after HTX's $8M drain (recovered).
- Lesson: When hunting CEX/bridge programs, trace operator and hot-wallet keys as an attack surface: one reused credential across affiliated entities lets an attacker "rinse and repeat" after the first breach. Follow post-breach fund-flows to accumulation wallets to link multi-incident campaigns.
- Aftermath: REKT highlighted all three Sun-linked hacks (Poloniex, HTX, HECO) as suspected Lazarus Group work with the same hallmarks — $233M lost across 4 incidents in 3 months. Sun promised full compensation of HTX hot-wallet losses within two hours.

## Hedera — Mar 10, 2023 — ~$515k stolen / ~$12M ecosystem damage
- Chain/Attack vector: Hedera Hashgraph; exploit of a flaw in the network's own Smart Contract Service code, used against HTS (Hedera Token Service) tokens across multiple DEXs' liquidity pools.
- Root cause: A vulnerability in Hedera's Smart Contract Service — specifically the decompiled-code path used by Uniswap-v2-derived DEX contracts ported to HTS — let the attacker call token functions on pool-held tokens as if authorized, effectively stealing authorization to burn and withdraw HTS tokens.
- Mechanics: The attacker targeted LP accounts on Pangolin, HeliSwap and other HTS DEXs; initially believed to affect only Hashport-wrapped tokens, it turned out ALL HTS tokens were at risk. The attacker burned bridged/wrapped tokens and removed LP positions; funds were bridged back to Ethereum via Hashport until it was deactivated, after which CEX cash-outs were used. Direct haul ≈$515k (Pangolin $120k, HeliSwap $2k; ~$60k HBAR + $280k HTS stables left on Hedera, $175k ETH on Ethereum), while TVL fell from $36.8M to $24.6M.
- Lesson: Chain-level smart-contract-service bugs make every DEX on the chain vulnerable at once — when auditing protocol contracts that wrap chain-native token services (HTS, Move objects, etc.), treat the platform token-service implementation itself as in-scope attack surface. A network pause is an operational kill-switch finding, not a fix.
- Aftermath: Hedera's governing council (Boeing, Dell, Ubisoft) turned off network proxies, taking mainnet fully offline — a first-of-kind response that REKT flagged as damaging DeFi legitimacy claims. HTS had been audited by FP Complete in 2021; the bug shipped in a smart-contract service update.

## Hedgey Finance — Apr 19, 2024 — $44.7M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + Arbitrum; flash-loan-funded abuse of the Token Claim contract's user-supplied parameters (not a reentrancy, a pure input-validation failure).
- Root cause: Missing validation on user parameters in createLockedCampaign — the attacker controlled the claimLockup/beneficiary fields, so the contract approved a token transfer to the ATTACKER's contract instead of the intended claimant.
- Mechanics: With a $1.3M USDC Balancer flash loan, the attacker crafted a createLockedCampaign call whose manipulated claimLockup parameter tricked the vulnerable contract (0xbc452fdc… on both chains) into approving USDC to the attack contract, then spent that approval to pull USDC. Run as two separate transactions per chain to dodge front-run bots. Ethereum lost $2.1M (USDC, NOBL, MASA) and Arbitrum lost $42.6M in BONUS tokens ≈ $44.7M total; funds parked at attacker-held addresses.
- Lesson: For every "create campaign / claim / vest" function, model the authorization flow explicitly — approval targets must be derived from validated campaign state, never from raw calldata parameters. Audit all multi-parameter token infra functions with a property test: no user input may redirect the approval recipient.
- Aftermath: Consensys Diligence had audited the Token Lockup and Vesting Plans in Jun-Jul 2023. Hedgey's team posted an onchain message to the attacker praising the "white hat" find ("well done") — a response REKT mocked as insulting to users; Cyvers first reported, Hedgey confirmed hours later.

## Hope Finance — Feb 20, 2023 — $1.86M
- Chain/Attack vector: Arbitrum; insider rug pull engineered through an upgradable SwapHelper parameter pointed at a fake router.
- Root cause: Governance-trusted parameter tampering — the team's 3/3 multisig approved a SwapHelper upgrade that swapped the real router for a fake one and set the swap-destination variable to the rugger's wallet, siphoning funds through the normal trade path (no smart-contract vuln in the pool itself).
- Mechanics: Pre-launch, the team deployed a fake router (txn 0xf188) and pointed SwapHelper at it (txn 0xc9ee, approved by all 3 multisig owners); _swapExactTokenForTokens was set to wallet 0x957D (txn 0x1b47). When GenesisRewardPool.openTrade() borrowed USDC, the pool sent WETH to TradingHelper "to swap" — the fake path instead forwarded it (and 477 WETH via swapExactTokensForTokens, with _uSDC deliberately left empty) straight to 0x957D. ~$800k WETH + ~$1M USDC drained at launch; USDC was swapped to a total of 1,095 ETH, bridged to Ethereum via Celer, and deposited into Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: An audit that never reviews the multisig's upgrade path and its current onchain parameters misses the most common rug mechanism — always diff the deployed SwapHelper/router pointers and their timelock/multisig ownership against the audited snapshot. Tomb-fork "GenesisRewardPool" clones with pre-launch router swaps are a red flag by themselves.
- Aftermath: The team's own tweet accused a named, KYC'd dev ("FUCKING SCAMMER… 2 MLN DOLLARS") whose ID's address is a vacant lot per Streetview — REKT notes fake/bought KYC is trivial and the whole team's multisig approved the rug tx. Audits by Cognitos (passed, two unrelated 'major' flags) and AuditRateTech (report later deleted).

## HTX (Huobi) — Sep 25, 2023 — $7.9M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; CEX hot-wallet drain (details scant, as usual for CEX hacks).
- Root cause: Hot-wallet key compromise on HTX (formerly Huobi); no technical details released, REKT flags the suspicious partial drain (4,999 ETH taken, 633 ETH left and "secured" two hours later) and delayed response as evidence of an inside job or sloppy key custody.
- Mechanics: At 10:00 UTC Sunday, 4,999 ETH (~$7.9M) was drained from an HTX hot wallet; HTX moved the remaining 633 ETH (~$1M) to safety over two hours later. Attacker addresses: 0xdb1D7446… and 0x799982b7… — announced by Justin Sun less than 12 hours after Mixin Network admitted a $200M loss.
- Lesson: CEX hunts should track hot-wallet anomaly patterns (partial drains, near-round amounts like 4,999 ETH, slow remaining-fund rescue) as insider/compromise indicators — the same signature shows up in the HECO/HTX Nov 2023 hacks. Cross-platform incident clustering (Mixin, HTX, JPEX within days) is campaign telemetry, not coincidence.
- Aftermath: Sun called the loss "two weeks' revenue" against "$3B held", offered the hacker an onchain 5% bounty plus a job (7-day deadline, then law enforcement), and claimed the hacker was "identified" without proof — the rebrand to "HTX" drew immediate FTX comparisons from CZ.

## Hundred Finance — Apr 15, 2023 — $7.4M
- Chain/Attack vector: Optimism; flash-loan exchange-rate donation attack on an EMPTY, unused hWBTC market of a Compound fork, amplified by a redeem rounding error.
- Root cause: exchangeRateMantissa in getAccountSnapshot was derived from the raw WBTC balance of the hWBTC contract, so a donation to the empty market inflated the rate — combined with a rounding error in redeemUnderlying that let tiny hWBTC balances redeem/borrow against a hugely inflated collateral value. The project had set up TWO wBTC cTokens (one UI-used, one empty) — the empty one was the hole.
- Mechanics: The attacker flash-loaned 500 WBTC from Aave, redeemed 0.3 staked WBTC, then with a second contract minted 200 hWBTC with 4 BTC, redeemed the 4 BTC back keeping a dust amount of hWBTC, and donated 200 WBTC to inflate the exchange rate so 2 wei of hWBTC controlled the pool. With ~2 remaining hWBTC the contract borrowed 1,021.91 ETH, repaid old debt with 1 hWBTC, and withdrew its 500.3 WBTC. Funds bridged to Ethereum (~$5.4M parked there, $0.9M left on OP); HND token fell ~50% intraday.
- Lesson: In Compound-style forks, EVERY cToken market — even an empty, UI-unused one — is an attack surface; donation-inflation works whenever exchangeRate reads live token balance and redeem/borrow rounding is sloppy. Inventory all deployed markets and fork-constant/rounding diffs against the original Compound math, not just the ones the UI exposes.
- Aftermath: Hundred's 3rd REKT entry (after the Meter stray hit $3.3M and the xDAI/Agave $6.2M reentrancy) — total $16.9M; the team called it "a general flaw in the code, not specific to Hundred" and offered $500k for info after onchain negotiation failed. Audited by WhiteHatDAO in Feb 2022; analysis by Von Fange/Peckshield/Beosin/Numen.

## Humanity Protocol — Jun 8, 2026 — 447M $H (team-confirmed; ~$36.4M realized)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + BSC; private-key leakage via DPRK-style spear-phishing of a director's laptop, then legitimate admin actions (hot-wallet drain, ProxyAdmin seizure, malicious bridge upgrade, unlimited mint).
- Root cause: Seven production private keys (3/6 ETH Safe owner keys, 3/5 BSC Safe owner keys) were backed up on ONE developer laptop a year earlier during mainnet setup and never rotated; the ProxyAdmin had NO timelock — so crossing the Safe threshold meant instant total control of both the bridge and token logic. The 6-signer multisig was theater: keys were spread across only 4 people, threshold was 3, and 3 keys lived in a single MetaMask.
- Mechanics: Director Chong Yee Wai opened a fake Bithumb zip on Jun 5 (hncagent.exe loader, Hancom-signed), giving the attacker RDP access; neither Sophos nor Defender flagged it. On Jun 8 the attacker drained 6,045,060 $H straight from the admin hot wallet, then with 3/6 ETH Safe keys transferred ProxyAdmin ownership, upgraded the bridge to a malicious implementation and swept 141,182,632 $H in one tx. On BSC the same playbook (3/5 keys) activated an unlimited mint: team confirmed 3×100M H mints; QuillAudits documented ~1.5B H total across undisclosed mints. ~$36.4M realized through Uniswap DEX-only dumping (never a CEX); $H fell ~90%; bridges halted, BSC contract still attacker-controlled.
- Lesson: For any audited protocol, verify the human layer behind the "multisig" — distinct signers per key, no key material ever co-located on one device, rotation history — and check EVERY ProxyAdmin for a timelock plus AdminChanged event monitoring; an upgrade path without a delay window converts one laptop compromise into instant protocol ownership. DPRK intrusions now start from spear-phished workstations: treat signer laptops as part of the attack surface.
- Aftermath: PeckShield called it "a staged performance… nothing less than a Rug Pull" (dev, MM-financier and MM keys on the same machine), while ZachXBT demanded disclosure of HK market-maker agreements then walked back to "MM activity and the compromise were independent". Specter flagged the drain first; recovery portal re-pegs H 1:1.048 against a snapshot taken 7 minutes before the first attack tx; 88% of the 9M registered Human IDs had never completed biometric verification.

## Hyperbridge — Apr 13, 2026 — ~$2.5M (revised; ~$806k direct)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; forged MMR (Merkle Mountain Range) proof accepted by the bridge's Solidity verifier — proof-verification logic flaw, not broken cryptography.
- Root cause: Missing bounds check in MerkleMountainRange.VerifyProof/CalculateRoot (Polytope Labs' own solidity-merkle-trees library): the verifier never enforced leaf_index < leafCount. With leafCount=1 and leaf_index=1, the single-leaf early exit was bypassed, no leaf fell in any subtree, and the function pushed proof[0] straight onto the peak-roots stack — so setting proof[0] == expected root made any fabricated message verify. No proof-to-request binding either (BlockSec's first read: MMR proof replay).
- Mechanics: The attacker submitted a ChangeAssetAdmin request for the bridged DOT token with leafCount:1, leaf_index:1, proof:[root], leafCount:1 — the computed root equalled the expected root without ever using the attacker's leaf hash. Admin of the DOT token contract (0x8d010bf9…) passed to 0xc513e4f5…, which minted 1B DOT (also ~999B ARGN, plus MANTA/CERE targets) and dumped into shallow pools for 108.2 ETH ≈ $237k. A separate earlier actor used the same door via TokenGateway for 245 ETH (~$570k, split 16.39 ETH × 15 wallets → Tornado Cash). Hyperbridge later revised total losses to $2.5M including incentive pools on ETH/Base/BNB/Arbitrum and users who swapped into artificially cheap DOT.
- Lesson: Proof-verification libraries are where "trustless bridges" actually live or die — fuzz every MMR/Merkle verifier for boundary inputs (leaf_index == leafCount, empty proof, proof == root) and enforce proof-to-request binding to kill replay. When auditors explicitly recommend "specialist review of your custom Solidity libraries", the next exploit candidate is exactly that library.
- Aftermath: Twelve days earlier Hyperbridge had posted an April Fools joke that Lazarus drained $37M, linking "Why Hyperbridge Can't Be Hacked" (deleted after the real hack). SR Labs' June 2024 audit had flagged the custom libraries and named "exploit a bug in validation scheme" as a high-value threat; a January 2025 advisory on the ismp-grandpa crate (verifier accepting invalid inputs) was the same bug class. The day after, Polkadot SDK's pallet_mmr disclosed the same-class bug (could have slashed ~446M DOT via forged BEEFY reports).

## Hypr Network — Dec 13, 2023 — 2.57M HYPR (~$220k)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; L2 bridge exploit via reinitialization of the OP Stack bridge contract — a known OP monorepo bug shipped because Hypr deployed an unreleased `develop` branch.
- Root cause: The `clearLegacySlot` modifier let the attacker reinitialize the (proxy) bridge contract, resetting the initialization guards and circumventing the `finalizeERC20Withdrawal` check. The underlying bug was a critical vulnerability in OP Stack's develop branch, patched by the OP team only AFTER Hypr's contracts were deployed.
- Mechanics: Two days after launch, the attacker called the vulnerable reinitialization path on the Hypr bridge and finalized a withdrawal of 2.57M HYPR tokens it didn't legitimately own. Proceeds were dumped for 97 ETH (~$220k), crashing HYPR ~40% intraday; only two addresses had bridged funds at that point, and the team initially told users the bridge was down "for more tests" before admitting the exploit. Attacker funded via FixedFloat; ETH sat at 0x5b8d… at writing.
- Lesson: Never ship a fork of a production `develop`/main branch — pin to governance-approved releases only, and check the upstream repo's security advisories/patches against your deployed bytecode at deploy time. Track "legacy slot clearing" and initializer-guard patterns in every upgradeable bridge you audit: a reinitialization path that resets guards is a full bypass candidate.
- Aftermath: First code-exploit bridge hack since the BNB Bridge (excluding Shibarium's self-rekt). OP Labs admitted the release/comms process gap and the Optimism Foundation said production builders should only use governance-approved releases; BlockSec published the technical summary and Hypr posted a full post-mortem.

## Indexed Finance — Oct 14, 2021 — $16M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; flash-loan-driven manipulation of the index pool's value oracle, a Balancer Pool fork whose reweighting is deliberately rate-limited.
- Root cause: extrapolatePoolValueFromToken priced the whole pool off "the first fully-initialized token with target weight > 0" (total_value = token_balance * total_weight / token_weight), and weight updates were enacted gradually to control slippage — so a manipulated token balance combined with the delayed weight update created a temporary, gameable pool valuation.
- Mechanics: The attacker flash-loaned the pool's other assets and used them to buy UNI, whose weight decrease hadn't yet taken effect, collapsing the extrapolated pool value (trades were capped at 1/2 of balance out / 1/3 of balance in, so this was done gradually). After calling updateMinimumBalance with the gamed value, the controller valued the DEFI5 pool at just 29,851 SUSHI (~$300k) despite it holding over $100M of assets. Depositing the now heavily over-weighted SUSHI then minted a massively inflated number of DEFI5/CC10 index tokens, which were cashed out for the pool's real assets — $16M across DEFI5, CC10 and (via its 37.05% exposure) the FFF pool; funds remained parked at the attacker address at writing.
- Lesson: In Balancer-style pools, audit EVERY valuation path that trusts an on-chain token balance or weight state mid-reweight — flash loans can move balances faster than weight updates land, and any single-token-extrapolation formula is a manipulation target. Bound minted index tokens by cross-checking the pool valuation against a second, manipulation-resistant source (time-weighted weights, minimum balances, NAV from multiple tokens).
- Aftermath: The team publicly believed they knew the attacker: a Discord user 'UmbralUpsilon' who, a month before, had asked detailed questions about the oracle parameters under the cover of "building an arbitrage bot" — and who'd requested Kovan testnet ETH via Gitter under a dead Twitter account. Dr Laurence Day offered a 10% white-hat bounty and warned of law enforcement; the exploit had sat undiscovered for ten months in production.

## Indodax — Sep 10-11, 2024 — ~$25.2M
- Chain/Attack vector: Multi-chain CEX breach (Ethereum, Polygon, Tron, BTC, Optimism); compromise of the withdrawal system itself rather than a plain hot-wallet key leak.
- Root cause: Per SlowMist, the attackers breached Indodax's withdrawal engine, which let them sign legitimate-looking withdrawals complete with change addresses that deposited back into the exchange — the withdrawals passed the exchange's own internal validation. Classic CEX custody failure (hot-wallet keys + withdrawal auth in one trust zone).
- Mechanics: Cyvers flagged suspicious outflows from Indodax wallets across networks; over 150 transactions drained 6.14M USDT, 1,047 ETH ($2.48M), 25 BTC ($1.41M), 2.2M MATIC ($849k), 1.4M ARB ($749.6k), 2M ENA ($465k) and more ≈ $25.22M total. Stolen funds consolidated at ETH ($12.37M), Polygon ($6.8M), Tron ($2.55M), BTC ($1.4M), Optimism ($900k) addresses while the exchange kept >$400M in other wallets. Indodax called it "complete maintenance"; co-founder William Sutanto claimed funds "100% safe".
- Lesson: When auditing a CEX program scope, model the withdrawal path end-to-end (auth → signing → hot-wallet custody → change-address handling): attackers who control the withdrawal system don't need the keys. Monitor for the Lazarus signature — multi-chain parallel draining, change addresses returning dust to the source, speed and precision — as an early detection heuristic.
- Aftermath: Cyvers' Yosi Hammer said the speed/complexity/pattern "highly resembles" Lazarus Group though unconfirmed. REKT's writeup arrived while the exchange was still in "maintenance" with the post-mortem pending; Arkham showed wallets still holding $400M+.

## Infini — Feb 23-24, 2025 — $49.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; rogue-privilege drain — a developer (0xc49b5e5b…) retained a special withdraw role (0x8e0b per QuillAudits) on Infini's vault and executed it after 114 days of waiting; no flash loans, no logic bug.
- Root cause: Access-control hygiene failure: the founder "was negligent when transferring authority" — privileges were never revoked when the developer finished work, with no mandatory privilege transfer, no time-based expiration, and no multisig requirement on critical withdrawal functions. The vault was also an unverified contract, so nobody audited the role graph.
- Mechanics: The attacker funded a fresh wallet with 1 ETH from Tornado Cash, deployed a contract disguised as part of Infini's vault system with admin privileges, then in two transactions withdrew 11.5M and 38M USDC from MEV Capital's Usual USDC vault on Morpho. USDC was swapped to DAI via Maker's Sky to dodge blacklisting, then DAI to 17,696 ETH at ~$2,798, parked at 0xfcc8Ad91… LookOnChain spotted the anomaly first ("a newly created wallet spent 49.5M $DAI to buy 17,696 $ETH").
- Lesson: Audit the COMPLETE role graph of every vault — who holds each privileged role today, whether any role outlives its need, and whether revoke/renounce exists and was called. Unverified contracts + lingering single-key withdraw roles is the combination to flag first; also treat "authority handover" commits in git history as a hunting lead.
- Aftermath: Christian (Infini founder) confirmed his personal key wasn't leaked and took personal responsibility, offering the attacker 20% + no legal action; 70% of the funds reportedly belonged to big investors he knew. The hack landed right after Bybit's $1.43B loss; QuillAudits noted the attacker used minimal obfuscation, so the trail may still be traceable.

## Inverse Finance — Apr 2, 2022 — ~$15.6M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; oracle manipulation — the INV price feed was a SushiSwap TWAP on a thinly-traded pair (short sample window), pushed 50x via a single 500 ETH → 1.7k INV swap and HELD there across blocks with MEV tricks.
- Root cause: Relying on a TWAP oracle generated from a single low-liquidity DEX pair with a short time sample — the classic Chainlinkgod critique — letting one big swap permanently inflate the collateral price. No secondary price validation against deeper venues.
- Mechanics: The attacker withdrew 901 ETH from Tornado Cash, dusted 241 clean addresses with 1.5 ETH via Disperse, deployed 5 contracts (one real), and swapped 500 ETH → 1.7k INV through SushiSwap's INV-WETH pair, moving the price ~50x on low liquidity. Simultaneously spamming the exploit transaction to land in the next block before arbitrage bots could correct the price, they deposited the 1.7k INV (fair value $644k) as collateral and borrowed $15.6M — 1,588 ETH, 94 WBTC, 4M DOLA, 39.3 YFI — and never repaid.
- Lesson: For lending protocols, map every collateral's oracle to its liquidity depth: TWAPs over thin pairs with short windows are the #1 manipulation target, and the fix is a deeper-liquidity/Chainlink-style feed, not a longer window alone. Watch for the MEV-aware signatures (Disperse-funded decoy addresses, cross-block price pinning, front-runner protection) as evidence of a pro attacker targeting your oracles next.
- Aftermath: Flashbot expert bertcmiller called it "one of the most MEV aware hacks I've seen" — the attacker held the oracle price across multiple blocks and blocked arb bots. Inverse announced a move to a Chainlink INV feed once liquidity requirements were met.

## Inverse Finance (2) — Jun 16, 2022 — $1.2M attacker / $5.8M total
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; second oracle manipulation in two months, this time via the DOLA market's yvcrv3Crypto collateral — flash-loan swaps moved the underlying Curve pool balances that the oracle priced directly.
- Root cause: The Inverse oracle "misused the balances of assets in the pool to directly calculate the LP token price" — pricing yvCurve-3Crypto from the live token balances of the Curve pool (not a manipulation-resistant quote), so any flash-loan-sized swap moved the collateral value up or down at will.
- Mechanics: Flash loan of 27,000 WBTC from Aave → deposit 225 WBTC to crv3crypto (5,375 crv3crypto) → 4,906 yvCurve-3Crypto into Inverse as collateral → swap 26,775 WBTC to 75,403,376 USDT to pump the pool's balance-based price → borrow 10,133,949 DOLA (far above the true collateral value) → reverse-swap USDT back to 26,626 WBTC, swap DOLA to 3Crv, redeem 10.1M USDT, buy 451 WBTC, repay the loan. ~1,000 ETH of profit went to Tornado Cash; the DOLA market was left with $10.63M bad debt (over half the protocol's $20M TVL).
- Lesson: Never price a yield-bearing LP token from the raw balances of its underlying pool — balance-based LP pricing is exactly the "second manipulation" Inverse suffered; require external or TWAP-based LP valuation. After any oracle incident, sweep EVERY other market in the same protocol for the same pricing pattern, since attackers return to the unfixed siblings.
- Aftermath: Peckshield disclosed the vuln publicly while funds were still at risk and deleted the tweet after being reprimanded. Peckshield later suggested the malicious tx may have been sniped by a front-running bot; Inverse claimed "no user funds were taken or at risk" while the attacker address was Tornado-funded two minutes before the exploit.

## Ionic Money — Feb 4, 2025 — ~$6.9M
- Chain/Attack vector: Mode Network (Ethereum L2); fake-collateral listing via social engineering — attackers impersonated Lombard Finance team members to get a counterfeit LBTC token approved as collateral, then unlimited-minted it.
- Root cause: Collateral onboarding that trusted humans over code: the team whitelisted a fake LBTC (0x964dd444…) whose mint function the attackers fully controlled, with a "legitimate" oracle/balancer pool/bridge story that passed their listing checks. No verification of token ownership or mint authority against the real Lombard deployment.
- Mechanics: Starting from 0.01 ETH, the attacker minted 250 LBTC from the counterfeit contract, supplied it to Ionic and borrowed MBTC, uniBTC, wrsETH, WETH, STONE and everything else across the protocol's vaults. 1,204 ETH (~$3.2M) was bridged to Ethereum and pushed into Tornado Cash; the attacker still holds 38.34 MBTC of loans (~$3.7M) on Mode. Blast radius extended to Ironclad and Layerbank, which held the borrowed MBTC positions.
- Lesson: Collateral-listing workflows need machine-checkable identity: verify the token's deployer/mint authority, compare bytecode with the canonical issuer, and require the issuer's known keys to sign the listing request — social engineering only works where a human decides "looks legit". Always simulate worst-case: if this token's mint is unlimited and held by the lister, the market is a free printer.
- Aftermath: ZachXBT noted Ionic is just Midas (twice-hacked in 2023: $660k + $600k) wearing a carnival mask; the team called it "quite a sophisticated social engineering exploit" while a Discord member asked what social engineering is. Lombard Finance confirmed "an unofficial LBTC has been deployed as collateral on Ionic Money on Mode."

## IoTeX (ioTube bridge) — Feb 21, 2026 — $4.4M drained + 821M CIOTX minted
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum bridge (ioTube); single-EOA owner key of TransferValidatorWithPayload compromised → malicious upgrade() → full bridge administration. No code exploit.
- Root cause: One EOA (0x6dd31a52…) held unchecked upgrade authority over the validator contract that guarded TokenSafe and MinterPool — no multisig, no timelock, no circuit breaker. The upgrade path itself was the master switch: the attacker pushed a validator stripped of every signature check, and the contracts obeyed. (IoTeX called it a "sophisticated, long-planned attack" with 6-18 months of preparation.)
- Mechanics: The attacker called upgrade() on TransferValidatorWithPayload (0xE7eBA1CE…) to install a validation-free implementation, then transferred ownership of TokenSafe and MinterPool. In 189 transactions on a Saturday morning: ~$4.4M of real reserves drained (1.36k USDC, 1.14k USDT, 635 WETH, 6.12 WBTC, 20,159 DAI, 8.72 PAXG, 13.85M IOTX, 45,825 BUSD, 2,835 UNI) and 821M CIOTX + 9.3M CCS minted across 7-10 txs (IoTeX's own 410M figure contradicted on-chain). Assets swapped to ~2,183 ETH, bridged via THORChain to 66.77 BTC parked in four fresh wallets, untouched. IOTX fell 22%; Upbit suspended deposits; chain patched to blacklist 29 attacker addresses.

## JayPegs Automart (Sushi MISO) — Sep 17, 2021 — $3.1M (returned)

## Jimbo's Protocol — May 28, 2023 — ~$7.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: Arbitrum; flash-loan attack on the semi-stablecoin rebalancing mechanism — no slippage control in the JimboController's shift() function.
- Root cause: Missing slippage/deadline protection in shift(), the rebalance path: the controller accepted deposits of over-valued JIMBO and moved WETH back into the pool at the attacker-manipulated price, letting the attacker cycle the imbalance for profit. V2 had just added leverage on top of the already-buggy v1 design.
- Mechanics: The attacker flash-loaned 10k ETH and bought JIMBO to pump its price, deposited part of the overvalued JIMBO into JimboController to trigger a rebalance via shift() (transferring the contract's WETH back into the pool), then sold the remaining JIMBO into the pool — draining all WETH liquidity and crashing the token. Over 4,000 ETH (~$7.5M) was bridged to Ethereum and parked at 0x5f3591e2…; the v2 protocol was only three days old (v1 had already collapsed at launch earlier in the month).
- Lesson: Every rebalance/peg-maintenance function needs explicit price bounds — slippage caps, deviation oracles, and reentrancy/deadline checks — because "deposit → trigger rebalance → sell" against the same pool is the canonical flash-loan kill chain. Protocols that relaunch a failed design with MORE complexity (leverage) are repeating the same flaw at higher speed; audit the new feature first.
- Aftermath: The team took ~6 hours to acknowledge the alert; Jimbo offered a 10% onchain bounty with a 4PM-UTC-next-day law-enforcement ultimatum, citing researchers from the Euler/Sentiment recoveries. REKT counted it as the 6th Arbitrum incident of 2023 and quoted UltraXBT's "sus v1 → v2 with even more complexity" take.

## Kannagi Finance — Jul 29, 2023 — ~$1.1M (of $2.1M TVL)
- Chain/Attack vector: zkSync Era; classic rug pull by the team/owner through the yield aggregator's admin withdrawal path.
- Root cause: Centralized ownership as designed: the audit report itself recorded that "the MainChef address can initiate a withdrawal on behalf of a user by specifying the user's address and an amount to withdraw" — the owner key was the vault. ## KelpDAO (rsETH / LayerZero DVN) — Apr 18, 2026 — ~$290M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + Arbitrum (Unichain→Ethereum OFT path); verification-layer breach — attacker poisoned LayerZero's own DVN infrastructure and forged a bridge packet; no smart-contract flaw in KelpDAO, Aave, or LayerZero's on-chain code.
- Root cause: Single point of failure by configuration: the rsETH Unichain→Ethereum OApp ran requiredDVNCount:1 / optionalDVNCount:0 with LayerZero Labs' own DVN as the sole verifier (LayerZero's documented default). Attackers breached LayerZero's trust boundary (root access to the internal RPC-node list), swapped op-geth binaries on two nodes, DDoS'd clean nodes to force failover, and served forged chain state exclusively to the DVN's IPs while appearing clean to everyone else; binaries self-deleted after. The DVN's 2-of-3 internal multisig then signed a fabricated packet (nonce 308: 116,500 rsETH "burned" on Unichain, where total supply was 49.26 rsETH); the endpoint's only check was hash equality, which passed.
- Mechanics: One forged packet released 116,500 rsETH (~18% of supply) from the shared custody adapter in a single tx; the attacker (funded with ~$230 from Tornado Cash ~10h earlier) split it across eight pre-staged wallets, supplied 89,567 rsETH to Aave V3 as collateral (eMode 3), and borrowed ~82,650 WETH + 821 wstETH at ~99% LTV within minutes — plus ~$40M on Compound/Euler before freezes. ~$178M consolidated on Ethereum, $72M on Arbitrum; the Arbitrum Security Council used emergency powers to freeze 30,766 ETH (~$73.6M), and ~75,700 ETH (~$175M) was converted to BTC via THORChain/Umbra/Chainflip within 36h. Blast radius: Aave WETH pools hit 100% utilization, $8.45B left Aave in 48h, ~$13.2B DeFi TVL erased, 30+ protocols paused LayerZero bridges, and Aave faces $123.7M–$230.1M bad debt depending on Kelp's loss socialization.
- Lesson: The bridge security config IS the audit: enumerate every OApp's requiredDVNCount/optionalDVN list on-chain and flag any 1-of-1 setup as critical — BGD Labs flagged exactly this risk for rsETH in Feb 2025 governance and it was disregarded. Infrastructure is now the attack surface: treat DVN/RPC/relayer operators (and their node binaries, failover logic, and signing quorums) as in-scope for bridge programs, and check for the RPC-poisoning signature — nodes that lie only to verifier IPs, DDoS-to-failover, self-deleting implants.
- Aftermath: Preliminary attribution is DPRK's Lazarus/TraderTraitor (2nd nine-figure theft in 18 days after Drift's $285M; $577M in 3 weeks). LayerZero blamed Kelp's 1-of-1 config; Kelp countered it was LayerZero's documented default — ~40% of LayerZero apps (1,111 of ~3,500 OApps) ran 1-of-1, and LayerZero only banned the config AFTER the hack. A "DeFi United" recovery effort (Stani 5,000 ETH, EtherFi, Lido, Mantle, Frax et al.) plus Kelp's 40,300 rsETH recovery left a ~89,500 ETH shortfall.

## KiiChain — Aug 22, 2026 — 148.3M KII (~$9.7M nominal; ~$1.6M realized)
- Chain/Attack vector: Cosmos EVM chain (KiiChain); exploit of a shared Cosmos EVM-module bug (staking precompile balance write-back), repeated against 18 victim wallets in one day — no KiiChain-specific code involved.
- Root cause: A linked pair of balance-accounting defects in the shared cosmos/evm module: a vesting-account underflow in the staking precompile's post-delegation balance write-back, plus a victim-balance overflow, letting an attacker redirect real tokens without creating new supply. The underflow is unreachable from a normal account (you can't delegate more than your spendable balance) — the attacker built a workaround to reach it.
- Mechanics: The attacker precomputed the future address of an EVM contract deployment, converted that address into a delayed-vesting account funded with exactly 2 KII, then deployed a helper contract onto it. The contract inherited vesting status, delegated 2 KII + 1 wei (one wei over its balance), and its mirrored EVM balance underflowed to ~2^256. Follow-up helper calls then debited chosen victim accounts (one drain: 1,023.953 KII; the largest: 42,178,466 KII) crediting the attacker — 148,326,583.15 KII across 18 sequences. The chain halted at block 9,355,723, freezing 54.4% (80.7M KII) in place; the rest was bridged to BNB via Hyperlane, ~95.6% of it sold on PancakeSwap (plus 3M KII to a KuCoin deposit) for ~$1.6M.
- Lesson: Chain-level EVM/state-machine accounting bugs propagate to every app-chain on the stack — when one chain (MANTRA, TAC) reports a native precompile exploit, immediately halt-and-audit sibling chains running the same module version. For hunt purposes: check vesting-account edge cases (delegate amount == balance + 1 wei, precomputed contract addresses converted to vesting accounts) as the exact repro path; watch upstream repos for fixes shipped without coordinated disclosure.
- Aftermath: Third chain hit by the same bug in 3 days (MANTRA Aug 20, TAC earlier Aug 22); Cosmos Labs' public acknowledgment came Aug 24-25, after private mitigation notices, and KiiChain accused the labs of knowing the flaw for months, treating it as low-risk, and shipping the fix without timely private warning. KII fell 83% intraday; KiiChain claimed "no user funds lost" after freezing over half the loot.

## KiloEx — Apr 14, 2025 — ~$7.49M
- Chain/Attack vector: Multi-chain perp DEX (Base $3.31M, opBNB $3.1M, BNB $903k, Manta $100k, Taiko $41k); oracle manipulation through a broken trusted-forwarder chain — forged signatures on MinimalForwarder.
- Root cause: Access-control gap in the keeper trust chain: KiloPriceFeed trusted Keeper, Keeper trusted PositionKeeper, PositionKeeper trusted MinimalForwarder — and MinimalForwarder verified neither the caller's identity nor the data, accepting any forged signature. Anyone could therefore impersonate the keeper and push arbitrary prices into the protocol's oracle (Pyth's data was fine; the implementation gate was the hole).
- Mechanics: A Tornado-funded wallet (0x00fac928…) forged the forwarder signature, cranked the ETH price down to $100, opened leveraged longs, then jacked the price to $10,000 and closed for profit — repeating across Base, BNB, opBNB, Taiko and Manta in quick succession for ~$7.49M total. Proceeds were bridged out via zkBridge, deBridge and Meson. KiloEx suspended trading, blacklisted the address, and offered the attacker a 10% "whitehat bounty" (keep 10%, return 90%) with silence in response.
- Lesson: For perp DEX audits, walk the ENTIRE price-feed trust chain end to end — keeper auth, forwarder/relayer signature verification, and what data the forwarder is allowed to relay; a single trustless link (MinimalForwarder) voids the oracle regardless of Pyth/Chainlink quality. Check the same chain on EVERY deployment: this exploit replayed 1:1 across five chains because the same hole was shipped everywhere.
- Aftermath: Five audits since June 2023 didn't stop it; the most recent auditor (ScaleBit, Mar 2025) said the root cause fell "outside the scope" of their audit. Chaofan Shou flagged the access-control flaw within minutes ("Anyone can change Kilo's price oracle"). KiloEx filed a Hong Kong police report and froze positions per pre-hack snapshots.

## KyberSwap (Elastic) — Nov 22-23, 2023 — $48M+
- Chain/Attack vector: Six chains (Arbitrum >$20M, Optimism $15M, Ethereum $7.5M, Polygon $3M, Base $2M, Avalanche $23k); flash-loan-driven tick manipulation of KyberSwap Elastic concentrated-liquidity pools exploiting a precision error.
- Root cause: Per BlockSec, "tick manipulation and double liquidity counting": swaps and position changes pushed each pool's active tick into empty regions of the liquidity curve, then cross-tick swap steps made the pool double-count liquidity, letting the attacker redeem far more than deposited. The underlying check failed by <0.00000000001% — a rounding/precision-scale failure in the tick math, exploitable only with surgically precise amounts.
- Mechanics: With a flash loan, the attacker drained the lowest-liquidity pools first, then used swaps + position edits to move current prices/ticks to the boundary where the vulnerable check's error flips sign. Multi-step swaps across ticks triggered the double counting and emptied the pools; the same recipe ran identically on all six chains from 0x50275e0b… (execution) with loot parked at 0xc9b826ba… (Scroll was funded but never attacked). TVL collapsed from $71M to under $3M. Funding: Tornado Cash on Ethereum (fanning out to ARB/OP/BASE) and FixedFloat for MATIC/AVAX.
- Lesson: Concentrated-liquidity tick math is a precision minefield — fuzz swap sequences at tick boundaries and empty-liquidity regions, and formally check the liquidity-counting invariant across cross-tick swaps (deltaLiquidity sums) rather than unit-testing happy paths. The attacker's own event-log comments ("Step 2, finding liquidity required…") show stepwise invariant probing is how pros find these — emulate it in audits.
- Aftermath: The exploiter left on-chain easter-egg comments and a message ("Negotiations will start in a few hours when I am fully rested") and sent 1,000 ETH to an address tied to the Oct 2021 Indexed Finance hacker (Andean Medjedovic). Elastic had been audited by ChainSecurity and a Sherlock contest; Spreek spotted the hack, confirmed by Kyber within the hour, and the month's total theft passed $300M.

## LastPass Users (wallet-draining campaign) — Oct 2023 (ongoing since Dec 2022) — $37M+ (incl. $4.4M in one day)
- Chain/Attack vector: Multi-chain, hundreds of individual EOAs; slow-burn wallet draining of seed phrases stored in LastPass vaults — no phishing, no contract exploit.
- Root cause: The 2022 LastPass breaches (Aug intrusion + Nov third-party cloud-storage breach where vault-data backups were copied) exposed encrypted vaults; attackers brute-forced/decrypted them. Per Tayvano the victims had "very secure" master passwords — implying either a still-undetected per-vault compromise method or undisclosed LastPass security details. Seed phrases in a cloud-synced password manager were the single point of failure.
- Mechanics: Since mid-Dec 2022, the attacker has drained hundreds of wallets with minimum losses "well over $10k" — victims include crypto-org employees, VCs, DeFi protocol builders and long-dormant (near-decade-old) wallets. Signature pattern: thefts cluster 10am-4pm UTC, favor weekends, tokens are swapped to ETH inside the victim's wallet before withdrawal, staked positions/NFTs are usually missed, and funds exit via non-KYC swap services (FixedFloat, SimpleSwap, SideShift, ChangeNOW, LetsExchange). One day last week produced $4.4M alone; total ≥$37M, traced by ZachXBT and Tayvano.
- Lesson: For bounty programs, "off-chain custody of user secrets" is a real attack surface: any integration that encourages storing seeds/keys in browser extensions or cloud password managers inherits their breach history — flag and suggest hardware/enclave storage. Hunt-wise, the campaign's on-chain fingerprint (UTC-hours clustering, weekend bias, in-wallet swap-before-send, CEX-less exits via instant swappers) is a reusable detection heuristic for wallet-drainer campaigns.
- Aftermath: The LastPass connection went public only in Aug 2023, though tracing began in April; LastPass' Aug 2022 statement claimed no customer data access, then the Dec update revealed vault backups were copied and warned of brute-force. REKT's takeaway: "Improved UX is not worth trusting sensitive info to 3rd parties… When did you last change your seed?"
