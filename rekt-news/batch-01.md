# REKT.news knowledge cards — batch-01

## Eleven Finance — 2021-06-23 — $4.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC (and Polygon), yield aggregator; flash-swap funded logic-flaw exploit of the Nerve "MasterMind" vault.
- Root cause: A vulnerable `emergencyBurn()` in the intermediate vault that tracked anySwap/Nerve-bridged assets (nrvBTC, nrvETH, nrvFUSDT) let the attacker withdraw the deposited balance without the withdrawal being accounted for internally — his deposit was counted twice.
- Mechanics: The attacker flash-swapped the vault's underlying tokens (pegged BTC/ETH/USDT) from PancakeSwap, minted them into nrvBTC/nrvETH/nrvFUSDT and deposited into the MasterMind vault via the intermediate vault, then called `emergencyBurn()` to pull out an amount equal to the vault's full pre-attack balance (i.e., his own deposit counted again), followed by a normal withdrawal of his original deposit. Total ~$4.5M: 30.75 BTCB (~$1.05M), 286 ETH (~$561K), 2.241M BUSD from the NRV 3Pool LP and 0.647M BUSD from the NRV-BUSD LP. He moved 2,293 ETH of proceeds out over the Nerve bridge to 0xdb2d590aCe7cAe51DF1fB3312738038Ec032Bf33.
- Lesson: Emergency/burn paths that bypass internal accounting must still reconcile balance-vs-share bookkeeping — hunt for any "withdraw without accounting" function in vault wrappers. When auditing an aggregator, prove that a deposit followed by burn + regular withdrawal cannot extract more than the depositor's own share.
- REKT narrative extras: Peckshield blamed a "dumb logic issue" — shockingly simple for $4.5M. The article entered the rekt leaderboard at position #27.

## 1inch (Fusion v1 resolvers) — 2025-03-05 — ~$5M (most returned; ~$450K kept)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; calldata corruption / integer underflow in the deprecated `_settleOrder` / `resolveOrders` (0x1944799f) path of 1inch's obsolete Fusion v1 resolver contracts.
- Root cause: A memory-pointer underflow from a crafted `interactionLength` of -512 (0xffff...fe00) redirected suffix data to a fake "interaction" structure, hijacking the resolver address. The flaw entered in November 2022 when 1inch rewrote the settlement code from Solidity to Yul; nine audit teams missed it, and Decurity — which actually spotted an integer-overflow risk in March 2023 — lost track of it when the code was refactored 24 hours later.
- Mechanics: The attacker (Tornado-Cash-funded deployer 0xa7264a43...) created normal-looking orders swapping pennies for millions, padded calldata with null bytes, set interactionLength to -512 so memory pointers underflowed and pointed at attacker-controlled data, and added a fake suffix "interaction" so the victim resolver (TrustedVolumes 0xb02f39e3...) got called as the resolution target. This drained 2.4M USDC + 1,276 WETH (~$5M) from resolver partners, including the 1inch Settlement V1 contract. After an on-chain "Can I have bounty?" negotiation with TrustedVolumes, he returned most funds and kept ~$450K (~10%).
- Lesson: Deprecated/legacy contract entry points that are still deployed and funded are live attack surface — retire or kill them, and re-audit after any language-level rewrite (Solidity→Yul) because audits of the pre-refactor source do not cover the deployed bytecode. Hunt for unconstrained user-supplied integers used as memory offsets/lengths in settlement or calldata-decoding code.
- REKT narrative extras: 1inch's first post said "no end-user funds at risk" and omitted the $5M; SlowMist exposed the real numbers the next day, and Decurity's Omar Ganiev published a candid postmortem confessing the firm's own missed tracking. The attacker accidentally sent half the haul to the 1inch settlement contract and had to race shoucccc and others to grab it back. 1inch launched a bug bounty program after the fact.

## 8ight Finance — 2021-12-08 — $1.75M
- Chain/Attack vector: Harmony; Olympus-DAO fork treasury drained via "compromised keys" (claimed multi-sig was never in place).
- Root cause: Operational key compromise of the treasury — no real multi-sig despite promises, plus the team's own key material mishandled (the devs' poor OPSEC was public knowledge).
- Mechanics: Three transactions drained the treasury: 490,170 DAI, then 378,417 DAI + 10,843 LP tokens, then 12,362 USDT (~$1.75M total). 868,587 DAI was consolidated from account xxca1d to cc541 and then sent to Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: Verify a protocol's claimed multi-sig/treasury custody on-chain before assessing risk — "compromised key" stories are usually missing or ceremonial multisigs. Key storage OPSEC (no keys in cloud docs/chats) is part of the attack surface you can audit from public artifacts.
- REKT narrative extras: Many users accused the developer of an inside job, noting the team still used Facebook-era OPSEC; rekt's verdict: "maybe hacked, maybe rugged, definitely rekt."

## LNDFi — 2025-05-09 — $1.18M
- Chain/Attack vector: Sonic; access-control backdoor in an Aave V3 fork ("Admin Privileges", attributed to a DPRK IT worker).
- Root cause: The deployer's modified aToken/VariableDebtToken contracts changed Aave's `onlyPool` modifier from `require(_msgSender() == address(POOL))` to `... || aclManager.isPoolAdmin(msg.sender)` — a five-word OR-clause that let any Pool Admin call `transferUnderlyingTo`, a function reserved for pool-internal plumbing in genuine Aave.
- Mechanics: On March 29, 2025 (41 days before the drain), in a 45-second window the deployer 0xc0454e29... granted himself the Pool Admin role and deployed the backdoored token contracts, then went dormant. At 02:29 UTC on May 9 the attacker called `transferUnderlyingTo` repeatedly to empty every pool: $476K USDC, 153.7 ETH ($389K), 373,594 wS ($202K), 189K staked Sonic ($105K) and 4.51 scETH ($11.5K) — $1,183,500 total — bridging out to Ethereum and BSC within ten minutes; admin rights were only revoked at 09:19 AM.
- Lesson: Diff any lending-protocol fork against the upstream reference implementation — a widened access modifier like `onlyPool || isPoolAdmin` is an instant critical. Admin/withdrawal privileges must be multisig + timelocked, and dormant-but-deployed backdoor modifications (weeks of staging) are exactly what on-chain monitoring should flag.
- REKT narrative extras: The team blamed an "undercover DPRK IT worker"; ZachXBT confirmed the DPRK attribution but was not formally engaged. Tiancheng Mai's unofficial postmortem exposed the backdoor that the official one glossed over, and the 41-day staging period remains unexplained.

## Lucky Star Currency / FSL — 2023-10-09 — $2.79M combined (two rugs)
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC; insider rug pulls via a privileged withdraw function and a pre-minted token dump.
- Root cause: LSC's deployer used the privileged `withdrawToken()` on its own AwardCentre and NFTMerge contracts to drain them; FSL was an unverified, <24-hour-old token contract whose deployer pre-minted himself 100M tokens at creation.
- Mechanics: In the early hours of Monday (UTC) the LSC deployer (0x9ef72ee6...) withdrew 1.65M LSC from AwardCentre and 1.45M LSC from NFTMerge, swapped the ~3M LSC for $1.1M BSC-USD and forwarded it to 0x23f8c805... ## Aave — 2026-03-10 — $27.78M liquidated (10,938 wstETH; no bad debt)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; liquidation storm caused by a protocol-level oracle configuration error (CAPO wstETH oracle), not an attacker.
- Root cause: The wstETH CAPO (Correlated Asset Price Oracle) had a stale anchor (~1.15, set in early 2024) plus a rate limiter capping updates at +3%/3 days; when Chaos Labs' Edge Risk engine pushed the correct snapshotRatio (~1.2282) with a 7-day-old timestamp, the contract clamped the ratio to ~1.1919 while the timestamp stayed 7 days back — so CAPO extrapolated a ceiling (~1.1939) below the live market rate (~1.2285).
- Mechanics: At 11:46 UTC on March 10 Chaos Labs computed the update off-chain; AgentHub (BGD Labs) executed it one block later via Chainlink Automation with no human review. Aave priced wstETH 2.85% below market, pushing 34 leveraged E-Mode (wstETH/WETH) positions past their liquidation line. Automated liquidators swept 10,938 wstETH ($27.78M), capturing ~512 ETH: ~116 ETH standard bonuses, ~13 ETH fees, and ~382 ETH pure arbitrage (buying at 1.1939, selling at 1.2285). Recoveries (~154.92 ETH) plus a TokenLogic AIP for a 512.19 ETH reimbursement (net ~357.56 ETH to the DAO) followed.
- Lesson: Safety-layer parameters that must move in lockstep (snapshot ratio vs timestamp vs growth cap) are a configuration invariant — a rate limiter that blocks legitimate correction is as dangerous as the manipulation it prevents. Audit stale-parameter drift on oracles/risk modules and simulate "cap clamped below market" scenarios; near-misses that fail to fire are pre-exploit warnings.
- REKT narrative extras: LTV Protocol spotted the anomaly before Aave's postmortem; the same mismatched update had almost fired a month earlier when the agent wasn't yet connected. It was the third act in a pattern (Moonwell's ~$1.78M cbETH bad debt, YieldBlox's $10M VWAP pool) of safety layers themselves mispricing, and came days after ACI and BGD Labs both exited the Aave DAO.

## Abracadabra (I) — 2024-01-30 — $6.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; MIM cauldrons drained via debt-share rounding + ERC-4626-style first-depositor share inflation on BentoBox.
- Root cause: A rounding issue in CauldronV4's `borrow` — the user's debt share (`part` of total debt) could be manipulated by repeatedly borrowing and repaying, combined with donating MIM to BentoBox to inflate the share price (the classic ERC-4626 first-depositor vector).
- Mechanics: The attacker flashloaned MIM via Degenbox, deposited MIM to BentoBox with the recipient set to BentoBox itself (donation), called `repayForAll()` to repay other users' liabilities (then manually repaid remaining borrowers to drive borrow elastic to zero), then looped borrow/repay to inflate the share price, added collateral, and borrowed a large MIM amount against the inflated shares. He drained the yvCrv3Crypto and magicAPE cauldrons for $6.5M, split across two accumulation addresses ($4.2M and $2.2M), and dumping the MIM for ETH depegged the stablecoin to ~$0.95.
- Lesson: Debt-share accounting (`part`/elastic) must be rounding-safe under borrow/repay loops, and vaults must defend against donation-based share-price inflation (dead shares / virtual shares). When auditing any fork of a lending vault, test first-depositor and single-user edge cases explicitly.
- REKT narrative extras: BlockSec and PeckShield raised the alarm within the hour; the team promised DAO-treasury buyback-and-burn of MIM to restore the peg and reached out to the attacker on-chain to negotiate.

## Abracadabra (II) — 2025-03-25 — $13M (6,260 ETH)
- Chain/Attack vector: Arbitrum; liquidation loophole in the GMX-linked gmCauldrons (failed GMX deposit + self-liquidation = phantom collateral).
- Root cause: A failed GMX deposit leaves the attacker's tokens claimable in GMX's OrderAgent; self-liquidating then wipes the position in Abracadabra but does not cancel the pending GMX order, so the protocol still counted the liquidated position as borrowable collateral.
- Mechanics: (1) Deposit into GMX but force the deposit to fail so tokens sit in OrderAgent; (2) borrow and push the position into liquidation; (3) self-liquidate — the contract wipes the position but forgets to scrub the order; (4) borrow again against the "ghost" collateral the system still sees. The attacker (0xAF9e33Aa...) drained 6,260 ETH (~$13M) from cauldron 0x625Fe795..., bridged Arbitrum→Ethereum and split it across three wallets. Abracadabra paused borrowing and offered a 20% bounty, unanswered.
- Lesson: Liquidation paths must invalidate every in-flight order/deposit in every integrated external system — cross-protocol state (GMX OrderAgent) is part of the invariant surface, not just the lending contract. Audit the failed-deposit + liquidation + re-borrow interaction explicitly; Guardian Audits owned the miss and added invariant testing in response.
- REKT narrative extras: Vladimir S ("Officer's Notes") flagged it first, PeckShield confirmed; GMX distanced itself ("no issues with GMX contracts"). The team's "Path Forward" promised a 6.5M MIM buyback, while its treasury is ~50% MIM/SPELL — cumulative Abracadabra losses now ~$20M.

## Abracadabra (III) — 2025-10-04 — $1.8M (1,793,755 MIM minted against zero collateral)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; logic bug in the deprecated CauldronV4 `cook()` multi-action flow — a status-flag reset bypassing the solvency check.
- Root cause: `cook()` executes bundled actions with a shared `CookStatus`; ACTION_BORROW (action 5) sets `needsSolvencyCheck = true`, but ACTION_CUSTOM (action 0) calls `_additionalCookAction()`, which "does absolutely nothing except return a fresh CookStatus(false)" — overwriting the flag so the final `if (status.needsSolvencyCheck) _ensureSolvent(user)` check is skipped.
- Mechanics: The attacker called `cook()` with actions `[5, 0]`: action 5 queued a massive borrow and set the solvency flag, action 0 immediately reset it, and the check never ran — 1,793,755 MIM borrowed against zero collateral across six deprecated mainnet cauldrons (last audited November 2023). About half was swapped to ETH: 51 ETH to Tornado Cash, then the rest (344 ETH) laundered through 3+ dozen mixer transactions. The DAO treasury bought back the dumped MIM.
- Lesson: Multi-action "cook"/multicall patterns must accumulate status flags (monotonic OR), never overwrite them — hunt for any action that can reset a security flag set by an earlier action. "Deprecated" contracts that still hold value and remain callable are production contracts: they need kill switches and re-audits, not labels.
- REKT narrative extras: William Li spotted it first, BlockSec Phalcon confirmed. Synnax (an Abracadabra fork with the same code) paused its contracts four days earlier, and PeckShield deleted the Synnax audit report 18 hours before the exploit and refunded the client — documented by William Li. Third exploit in under two years; cumulative ~$21M.

## Acala Network — 2022-08-14 — ~$1.3B erroneously minted (~$1.6M escaped)
- Chain/Attack vector: Polkadot (Acala parachain); infinite mint via a misconfigured liquidity-pool incentive — `incentives.claim_rewards()` on the new iBTC/aUSD pool minted aUSD exponentially (1.3B total).
- Root cause: The iBTC/aUSD pool, live the same day, was misconfigured so its reward-claim extrinsic minted aUSD in exponential amounts instead of paying configured rewards — an error-mint driven by pool config, not a contract exploit.
- Mechanics: One user's wallet minted ~1.2B aUSD from the bug (some users triggered it accidentally); a handful of others monetized it by bridging aUSD to Moonbeam, swapping to DOT (sent to Polkadot) and iBTC (sent to Interlay), nearly draining the iBTC pool. About $1.6M in "good value" and $4.6M of bad-debt aUSD left the chain before Polkadot governance disabled transfers; the accidental minter cooperated to return and burn his excess, and aUSD held above $0.90 after reactivation.
- Lesson: Reward/incentive payout functions must be bounded by their configured rate and unit-tested against pool config changes — a misconfigured pool reward rate is an unbacked-mint primitive. Treat "pool went live today" config as a security event: check reward math on bootstrapped pools before trading starts.
- REKT narrative extras: Acala called it "a misconfiguration of the iBTC/aUSD liquidity pool"; on-chain tracing of recipient addresses was underway and a community governance decision was pending on how to handle the wrongly minted aUSD. The 1.2B-mint wallet was contrasted with the accidental user who cooperated ("this was a fault of the system, not mine").

## Aevo / Ribbon Finance — 2025-12-12 — $2.7M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; legacy Ribbon (Opyn Gamma-based) options vaults — a Dec 6 oracle-config upgrade exposed the price-feed proxies, and the attacker exploited unprotected proxy-admin access plus an 18-vs-8 decimal precision gap.
- Root cause: The upgrade left `transferOwnership`/`setImplementation` effectively unprotected — the proxy admin checked a permission flag on `tx.origin` rather than the caller — letting anyone holding the flag swap oracle implementations and set arbitrary expiry prices. The rewritten config also dropped Opyn's strict collateral-matching rules and mixed 18-decimal with 8-decimal assets.
- Mechanics: A staged multi-wallet crew (Mastermind/Specialist/Engineer/Bagman/"Fall Guy") whitelisted collateral and deployed a disguised oracle to build a compliance history, then in one transaction hammered the Proxy Admin (0x9D7b...) to swap the oracle to a malicious implementation that cranked the AAVE price "to infinity" and locked the expiry timestamp to Dec 12, 2025. The attacker created "Frankenstein" oTokens (wstETH-collateralized, AAVE-underlying, stETH call with a 3,800 USDC 8-decimal strike) — e.g. burning 225 oTokens returned ~22.46 WETH per cycle — and the primary drain burned oTokens for 846 WETH + 178K USDC (~$2.6M). Proceeds were distributed in precise 100.1 ETH batches for Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: Any upgrade touching oracle/proxy access must be diffed against the original invariants (Opyn's collateral-matching was silently dropped), and proxy-admin authorization checking `tx.origin` or a permission flag instead of `msg.sender` is an instant critical. Legacy "zombie" vaults with live TVL need decommissioning kill switches, not maintenance patches.
- REKT narrative extras: Specter flagged the exploit before Aevo announced it (19 hours later). Aevo decommissioned all Ribbon vaults and deleted its compensation tweets after proposing a 19% haircut partly subsidized by "zombie" depositors dormant 2-4 years; the 2021 OpenZeppelin audit offered no protection post-upgrade.

## AFX Trade — 2026-07-22 — $24.15M USDC
- Chain/Attack vector: Arbitrum; validator compromise of the AFX bridge's off-chain signing system (no smart-contract bug).
- Root cause: Five hot-validator keys (7,142 of 10,000 voting power, above the 6,667 quorum) — the same validator set in place since deployment on May 12, 2026 — signed a fraudulent withdrawal. The 200-second dispute window was built to dispute invalid state, not compromised signers, and no key-management or per-withdrawal cap stood in the way.
- Mechanics: An operational address created the withdrawal, it cleared the dispute window, and a batched finalize released $24.15M USDC to the loot wallet (0x2f2974fa...). The funds moved like routine traffic: six transfers through the shared BridgingKit CCTP router (burn on Arbitrum, mint on Ethereum to the same recipient), then eight UniswapX Dutch-auction fills (solver "Rizzolver") swapped them to 12,467.44 ETH, consolidated into 0x627654B2..., then fragmented across ~24 addresses in a day. AFX's 30%-keep bounty went unanswered.
- Lesson: Bridge withdrawal authorization is key management: hot validator keys controlling a large quorum are the vulnerability, and audits of contract logic (Zellic documented zero test coverage and unresolved acknowledgments) say nothing about the signers. Require cold-storage/multi-domain custody, per-withdrawal caps, and dispute windows that react to validator-compromise indicators.
- REKT narrative extras: Blockaid flagged it at 21:30 UTC with the exact figure before AFX spoke; Arbitrum's Steven Goldfeder drew the boundary that the native bridge was not involved. zeroShadow/SEAL attribute it to UNC4899 (TraderTraitor, DPRK) via a gas-funding pattern matching the KelpDAO exploit; Ostium lost $23.75M on the same network a week earlier.

## Agave DAO & Hundred Finance — 2022-03-15 — $11.7M combined (2,116 ETH + 2,363 ETH)
- Chain/Attack vector: Gnosis (xDai); reentrancy through the xDAI token's `callAfterTransfer()` hook, against an Aave fork (Agave) and a Compound fork (Hundred Finance).
- Root cause: The xDAI token's `callAfterTransfer()` callback created a reentrancy primitive that neither fork guarded against when porting from Ethereum — nested borrows executed before the protocol updated the debt balance, letting the attacker borrow far more than his collateral.
- Mechanics: Using flash-loan collateral, the attacker nested borrow calls inside one another via the token's post-transfer callback so each borrow executed against the not-yet-updated debt state; repeating this drained 2,116 ETH ($5.5M) from Agave (11:25:40 UTC) and 2,363 ETH ($6.2M) from Hundred Finance (11:28:40 UTC), three minutes apart. Both hauls were bridged to the attacker's ETH address and sent to Tornado Cash within hours.
- Lesson: Forking a battle-tested protocol does not inherit its security properties — re-verify every environment-specific token behavior (chain-native tokens with hooks like `callAfterTransfer`) when porting code across chains. Reentrancy guards/CEI and strict collateral whitelists are the two mitigations the originals had and the forks dropped.
- REKT narrative extras: Same vector as CREAM Finance's $18.8M loss the previous August; AGVE plunged >20% while HND barely moved. Daniel Von Fange and Mudit Gupta credited for the analysis; entered the leaderboard at #35.

## Akropolis — 2020-11-12 — $2M DAI
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; reentrancy through a malicious ERC20 used as the deposit token, exploiting balance-delta deposit accounting (dYdX flash-loan funded).
- Root cause: The vault computed the deposit amount as the difference in token balance before and after the transfer, and accepted any token — a malicious token's transfer callback re-entered `deposit`, so the same balance delta was credited twice.
- Mechanics: The attacker created a fake token whose transfer re-enters deposit: deposit faketoken → callback fires → deposit 25K DAI inside the callback → credited 25K DAI from the delta, and also credited for the original deposit → withdraw 50K DAI. Batches of ~$50K were executed over ~8 hours before the main hit; ~$2M of gains were sent to a holding address.
- Lesson: Never derive deposits from before/after balance deltas when the deposited token is attacker-controlled — use share-based accounting with CEI and reentrancy guards, and whitelist deposit tokens. Treat "accepts arbitrary ERC20 as deposit token" as a reentrancy search surface.
- REKT narrative extras: The contracts had been audited by Smartdec and Certik; Akropolis admins initially claimed they were just doing "fixes" before the $2M loss came out — the lie cost them the community's trust.

## Alchemix — 2021-06-16 — ~2,700 ETH (~$6.5M) unbacked alETH
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; logic bug in the newly launched alETH (ETH-collateral) product — borrowers were assigned no debt and could withdraw their locked collateral while keeping the minted alETH.
- Root cause: When the DAI→alUSD transmuter design was replicated for ETH→alETH, the debt assignment was never applied: users who minted alETH had zero debt recorded, so their ETH was withdrawable while the alETH remained spendable.
- Mechanics: Users discovered they could mint alETH against deposited ETH and simultaneously withdraw the ETH that was supposed to stay locked as collateral — a self-serve "reverse rugpull" with no attacker needed. The team paused new alETH mints within hours, but the alETH pool was left undercollateralized by ~2,700 ETH (~$6.5M), which the treasury had to backstop via a public voluntary-return appeal.
- Lesson: Every new collateral type deployment must re-run the debt-accounting invariant tests (mint ⇒ debt = collateral value) and monitor the first blocks for zero-debt positions — a copied "transmuter" that skips debt assignment is a free-withdrawal bug. Pause switches on new products are cheap insurance.
- REKT narrative extras: Co-founder Scoopy Trooples told rekt the team had ~$4M non-ALCX treasury and needed >25% voluntary returns to stay solvent; one user re-invested the misappropriated funds straight back into the protocol.

## AlexLab (I) — 2024-05-14 — $4.3M
- Chain/Attack vector: BNB network; XLink bridge (ALEX/Stacks) drained after a phishing-compromised deployer key performed four malicious proxy upgrades.
- Root cause: Private key compromise (phishing) of the deployer address, which held proxy-admin rights — the attacker upgraded the bridge proxy so its endpoint pointed to unverified malicious bytecode, then drained the ALEX liquidity-pool vault.
- Mechanics: The deployer address executed four malicious upgrades; within an hour the attacker (0x27055aE4...) withdrew ~13.7M STX (~$4.3M) across two transactions, sending ~3M STX to centralized exchanges before freezes could land. The team recovered aBTC, sUSDT, xBTC, xUSD, ALEX, atALEX and other assets, and offered a 10% bounty with a 48-hour deadline; some STX slipped through CEX freezes.
- Lesson: The proxy-admin/deployer key is the highest-value key in the system — put upgrades behind a multisig with timelock and monitor for any implementation change. Audits (CoinFabrik) and bug bounties (Immunefi) don't protect against the upgrade path, so threat-model the admin surface as first-class.
- REKT narrative extras: Certik Alert caught the suspicious transaction May 14, but AlexLab confirmed only the next day. ImmuneBytes/Chain Aegis linked the attacker to the Mars Defi 412 price-manipulation attack ($100K, April 16); the team floated burning unrecovered STX and reissuing tokens to victims.

## AlexLab (II) — 2025-06-06 — ~$16.2M (official tally $8.37M)
- Chain/Attack vector: Stacks; vault permission hijack through the permissionless self-listing flow — a malicious "token" with a fake transfer function drained the amm-vault.
- Root cause: Lax vault access controls and misuse of `as-contract`: creating a legit-looking pool auto-triggered `set-approved-token` and granted vault permissions; enabling farming (flag 9) unlocked the malicious token's transfer; when the vault called the token's transfer with `as-contract`, the vault itself became the sender and the fake transfer drained every asset. The team's "Stacks can't detect failed transactions" excuse was wrong — it was AlexLab's own permission system.
- Mechanics: The attacker deployed "ssl-labubu-672d3" (a token with a draining transfer function), created a Labubu/STX pool to get vault permissions auto-granted, flipped the farming flag, then executed one `swap-x-for-y` call — the vault-as-sender context let the fake transfer sweep 8,403,867 STX ($5.54M), 50.74 aBTC ($5.43M), 12.76 sBTC ($1.35M), 119.4M ALEX ($2.12M) and 1.75M sUSDT ($1.74M) out of vault SP102V8P0...amm-vault-v2-01 in a single transaction.
- Lesson: Permissionless listing flows must never auto-grant vault-level permissions to user-supplied token contracts, and a vault must never call attacker-controlled code with an elevated (`as-contract`) context. Audits of the upcoming product (DAMM) don't cover the live system — audit what is deployed and in production, and check every path where user tokens get "approved" privileges.
- REKT narrative extras: Reubs BTC raised the alarm within minutes; researcher Nolan (Exvul) debunked the Stacks-blame framing. Official damage claims ($8.37M) were roughly half the on-chain total ($16.18M), and compensation was promised from the foundation treasury — the second AlexLab exploit in 13 months with a completely different vector.

## Alpha Finance (Alpha Homora) — 2021-02-13 — ~$37.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; debt-share rounding + permissionless `resolveReserve` + custom "evil spell" against HomoraBankv2's unannounced, contract-level-only sUSD pool.
- Root cause: Four stacked flaws: (1) an sUSD pool existed at contract level with zero liquidity and no announcement; (2) a rounding miscalculation in `borrow` when the attacker is the sole borrower; (3) `resolveReserve()` was callable by anyone and increased totalDebt without increasing totalDebtShare; (4) HomoraBankv2 accepted any custom spell as long as the collateral > borrow invariant held.
- Mechanics: The attacker deployed an "evil spell", borrowed 1,000 sUSD as the first (sole) borrower, then repaid all but 1 mini-sUSD so he held 1 debt share; calling `resolveReserve` accrued ~19,709,787,742,196 units of debt while totalShare stayed 1; he then looped borrows of (totalDebt − 1) — each rounding to 0 new borrow share, so the protocol treated them as debt-free — doubling 16 + 10 + 10 times, seeded with Aave flashloans (1.8M then ~10M USDC swapped to sUSD and deposited to Cream's Iron Bank for liquidity). The haul: 13,244.63 WETH + 3.6M USDC + 5.6M USDT + 4.26M DAI; stablecoins were wrapped into aTokens/a3Crv to evade freezes, and 1K ETH was sent to each of Cream's and Alpha's deployer addresses as a false flag.
- Lesson: Borrow share must never round to zero — enforce a minimum borrow or round shares up, and fuzz the first/sole-borrower edge case. Any permissionless function that mutates totalDebt without touching shares (like `resolveReserve`) is a debt-inflation primitive; also treat "deployed but not announced" pools as live attack surface.
- REKT narrative extras: The exploit required insider knowledge (the unannounced pool), so Alpha Finance named a "prime suspect"; SBF withdrew $400M FTT from Cream and 3AC sent $3M ALPHA to Binance amid the panic. Andre Cronje called it "9 transactions, 4 different manipulations", Banteg: "The setup is absolutely insane."

## AlphaPo — 2023-07-22 — $60M (ETH, TRON, BTC)
- Chain/Attack vector: Multi-chain CEX/payments-processor hot-wallet drain (alphapo.eth) — attributed to the Lazarus Group's distinct on-chain fingerprint, via spearphishing.
- Root cause: Compromised hot-wallet keys of a crypto payments processor serving gambling platforms; no smart-contract flaw — key management and hot-wallet concentration were the vulnerability.
- Mechanics: In the early hours (UTC) of Saturday, the alphapo.eth hot wallet was drained of 2,464 ETH ($4.6M) plus 6M+ USDT and assorted tokens on Ethereum; assets were swapped to ETH, consolidated into a secondary account and dispersed, while TRON-side funds were routed to CEXs. ZachXBT first reported $23M, then traced a further $37M, totaling $60M across ETH/TRON/BTC.
- Lesson: Hot-wallet operations are the attack surface to audit on any payments/CEX-adjacent service — hunt for large single-key wallets, missing withdrawal limits and no cold-wallet tiering, and watch for Lazarus fingerprint patterns (swap-to-ETH, consolidation, dispersion) when triaging any wallet-drain report.
- REKT narrative extras: MistTrack linked the wallets to addresses from the Atomic Wallet drain (also Lazarus) and a possible Coinspaid attack; Hypedrop, a customer, suspended deposits. rekt noted a $60M hack barely moved the news cycle anymore.

## Ankr & Helio — 2022-12-02 — $5M (Ankr) + ~$19M (Helio Money, via depegged aBNBc collateral)
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC; compromised Ankr deployer key → malicious upgrade of the aBNBc token with a public free-mint; cascaded into Helio Money through a stale oracle.
- Root cause: Phishing-compromised Ankr deployer address upgraded aBNBc to an implementation containing a new function (0x3b3a5522) that bypassed caller verification and minted tokens freely to the attacker — 60 trillion aBNBc ("18 quadrillion dollars" theoretical). Peckshield and Beosin audits had already flagged the privileged-account risk ("Confirmed"/"Acknowledged") and Ankr left it unfixed.
- Mechanics: The compromised deployer funded the exploiter (0xf3a465c9...) and published/upgraded the malicious implementation; the attacker minted directly to himself and drained PancakeSwap's aBNB pools — limited to ~$5M only by available liquidity — while copycats joined the public mint. A second actor bought the depegged aBNBc cheap and, before Helio's oracle updated, collateralized it to borrow 16M HAY (~$15.5M profit; another user ~$3.5M), depegging HAY to $0.62. Binance froze ~$3M of the proceeds.
- Lesson: Deployer/owner keys of yield-bearing receipt tokens are mint authorities — upgrades must be timelocked and multisig, and audit findings on privileged roles ("Confirmed" risk) must be remediated, not acknowledged. Oracle staleness turns one protocol's depegged token into the next protocol's free collateral: check downstream protocols' oracle latency for exactly this contagion.
- REKT narrative extras: CZ's summary: developer key hacked → contract upgraded to a malicious one → Binance paused withdrawals and froze ~$3M. Helio promised user compensation; underlying staked BNB was unaffected and aBNBc was reissued via snapshot.

## AscendEX — 2021-12-11 — $77.7M
- Chain/Attack vector: CEX hot wallet drained across three chains (Ethereum $60M, BSC $9.2M, Polygon $8.5M) — "compromised" keys, mechanism never explained.
- Root cause: Compromised hot-wallet custody on three chains at once; no technical detail ever released — the failure was operational key management, not code.
- Mechanics: Draining began just before 8 PM UTC on December 11 from the exchange's hot wallets on Ethereum, BSC and Polygon, with the stolen funds consolidated into attacker addresses on each chain. The BSC loot included Bemil Coin, which dumped 98% post-incident, cutting the BSC-side value by ~$6.5M. Withdrawals were suspended and full reimbursement was promised.
- Lesson: When a CEX incident spans multiple chains simultaneously, it points to a single custody/ops compromise — triage accordingly rather than hunting for per-chain bugs. Track whether a breached exchange actually makes users whole before tokens it must buy back lose value (the Bemil dump shrank the recoverable value by $6.5M).
- REKT narrative extras: Came the same week as BitMart's ~$196M loss; AscendEX deleted incriminating tweets and promised refunds. As one anon noted, the loss exceeded their entire Series B raise.

## Atlantis Loans — 2023-06-07 — ~$1M, rising to $2.5M a week later
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC; governance takeover of an abandoned-but-live lending protocol, then malicious contract upgrades draining anyone with lingering token approvals.
- Root cause: The team abandoned the protocol in early April but left it live ("changes must go through governance") with the UI prepaid for two years; nobody monitored proposals, so the attacker pushed and passed proposal #52 granting control of the token contracts and upgraded them to malicious versions that swept tokens from every address with an active approval.
- Mechanics: An earlier attempt (April 12) failed to pass; with the project dead, proposal #52 (published June 7) passed unnoticed. The attacker (0xEADe071F..., originally Binance-funded) upgraded the token contracts and drained ~$1M from users who still had approvals to Atlantis contracts; within a week further drains brought the total to $2.5M (per PeckShield).
- Lesson: Abandoned-but-live protocols are governance-attack targets — the lesson cuts both ways: as a hunter, audit dead projects for takeoverable governance (low quorum, cheap token) and always check old approvals; as a defender, a shutdown must actually revoke powers, not just stop maintenance. Governance needs execution delays and emergency vetoes even in sunset mode.
- REKT narrative extras: rekt listed the pattern: Tornado Cash's governance hijack, Beanstalk's $181M flash-loan governance attack (no execution delay), and Swerve's failed attempt. Numen Cyber published the full proposal-breakdown thread and the list of contracts users should revoke.
