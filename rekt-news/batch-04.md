## EasyFi — 2021-04-20 — ~$59M ($6M stables + ~$53M EASY)
- Chain/Attack vector: Polygon — compromised founder machine / leaked admin mnemonic keys (no contract vulnerability)
- Root cause: single admin key stored in a hot (MetaMask-style) wallet with no multisig, no hardware wallet, no timelock; when the founder's dedicated transfer machine was compromised the key fell into the attacker's hands.
- Mechanics: the hacker did not touch the code — they took the mnemonic controlling EasyFi's admin key on Polygon. With that one key they drained ~$6M in stablecoins plus ~2.98M EASY (~$53M, roughly 30% of total supply) via a plain `transfer` call that had no timelock. The token only fell ~20% because the thief could not easily dump: EASY's 24h volume was only ~$10M and the attacker now holds ~30% of supply. The protocol effectively became centralized in a criminal's hands overnight.
- Lesson: (1) Audit admin-key topology as a finding in itself: any single hot key that can drain treasury liquidity with no timelock is a Critical waiting to happen — require multisig + hardware wallet + timelock for anything holding other people's funds. (2) "No code bug" hacks still kill protocols: check OPSEC posture (key custody, machine isolation) in pre-audit recon.
- REKT narrative extras: founder AnkittGaur said the compromised machine was used "solely for official transfers"; team published an open letter offering the hacker a $1M "clean payout" bounty; rekt noted the OPSEC was "poor even just for an individual". First big Layer-2 hack.

## Euler Finance — 2023-03-14 — ~$197M (86k ETH derivatives, 849 WBTC, 34M USDC, 8.9M DAI)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — flash-loan + leveraged self-liquidation via `donateToReserves` missing a health check
- Root cause: `donateToReserves` (added in EIP-14) let users send eTokens straight to Euler's reserves without checking the donator's account health — allowing creation of unbacked dToken debt that could never be liquidated.
- Mechanics: attacker used two contracts: one built a huge, underwater leveraged position with flash-loaned funds, then donated eTokens to reserves, making the position unliquidatable while leaving the collateral claim inflated; the second contract acted as liquidator, seizing the inflated eToken collateral at a discount and withdrawing the underlying. Losses totaled ~$197M (stETH/rETH ~$134.6M, 849 WBTC ~$18.6M, 34M USDC, 8.9M DAI); Euler TVL fell $264M → $10M. Some funds were routed toward Tornado Cash via a pass-through address.
- Lesson: (1) Every state-changing path that moves collateral or debt — even a "donation" — must re-run the account-health invariant; donative/exempt paths are prime hunting ground in lending protocols. (2) Trace cross-protocol blast radius: integrated vaults (Angle $17M, Balancer $11.9M, Temple $5M) all lost through one missed health check.
- REKT narrative extras: Peckshield raised the alarm; auditor Omniscia (1 of 6 auditors) published the post-mortem and audit-insurance protocol Sherlock paid a $4.5M claim for missing the bug; Euler reached out to the attacker on-chain via tx input data but funds had already started moving to Tornado.

## Fei Protocol — 2021-04-07 — peg break / TRIBE -30% (design failure, not an exploit)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — economic-mechanism failure: "direct incentives" sell-penalty model + pre-swap launch mechanics collapsed the peg (no attacker)
- Root cause: FEI's below-peg burn penalty taxed both sides of every trade (Emin Gün Sirer: punishing sellers also punishes buyers, shrinking supply AND demand); on top, the launch's "pre-swap" auto-converting FEI→TRIBE created millions in guaranteed sell pressure, and a vulnerability found in the incentive calculation forced all minting rewards to be shut down.
- Mechanics: the Token Genesis Event offered up-to-50% mint discounts but the discount was voided once contributions exceeded $250M — they raised $1.3B in ETH, which went irreversibly into Protocol Controlled Value; ~17k addresses participated. PCV was used to build a Uniswap FEI/ETH pool that peaked at $2.6B in liquidity. The peg broke quickly under pre-swap selling pressure; because ~98% of TRIBE liquidity sat in the TRIBE-FEI pair, holders selling TRIBE for ETH were forced through the penalty or paid heavy slippage. Rewards shutdown followed the incentive-calculation bug despite Consensys Diligence + OpenZeppelin audits.
- Lesson: (1) Economic/mechanism audits must simulate both sides of every trade — penalties that burn on below-peg transfers tax the counterparty too and can kill demand exactly when the peg needs it most. (2) Check conditional launch discounts and pre-swap/auto-convert options: a threshold-crossing that retroactively voids a promised discount converts hype into a de facto rug.
- REKT narrative extras: backed by a16z, Framework and Coinbase yet widely called "an overfunded disaster"; Julien Bouteloup (rekt founder) posted a public "blacklist" of FEI holders to exclude from future airdrops; Robert Leshner publicly bid "$0.70 USDC" for unlimited FEI.

## Force DAO — 2021-04-04 — 4,112 FORCE drained & returned; -90% price crash
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — whitehat "rescue" mint via unchecked `transferFrom` return value in the FORCE/xFORCE vault
- Root cause: the xFORCE vault called `transferFrom` on the years-old MiniMeToken, which returns `false` instead of reverting when unapproved/underfunded; the vault ignored the return value (no SafeERC20 wrapper), so anyone could mint xFORCE without transferring any FORCE.
- Mechanics: a whitehat found the bug and minted 347,432,986 xFORCE, withdrew 4,112 FORCE with it and sold part via 1inch, then alerted the team's Discord at 08:50 UTC ("removed all of them for the safety of the token"). The community dumped before the warning landed — FORCE fell 90% in minutes — and copycat hackers with "poor OPSEC" then arrived to feed on the leftovers. The whitehat ultimately returned 14,833 FORCE, keeping Force DAO off the rekt leaderboard.
- Lesson: (1) Never trust ERC20 return values — legacy tokens like MiniMe return false instead of reverting; wrap every transfer/transferFrom in SafeERC20 and treat "false" as failure, especially inside mint/burn vaults. (2) The return-value bug class repeats across years (BAT-style); when reviewing vaults, check the exact token standard used, not just the function names.
- REKT narrative extras: samczsun's one-liner — "lmao the xforce vault didn't check the return value on transferFrom"; rekt dubbed it "the smallest airdrop followed by the easiest hack"; whitehat returned the money and the team narrowly avoided the leaderboard.

## Fortress Protocol — 2022-05-09 — $3M
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC — governance takeover (malicious proposal) + publicly callable price oracle manipulation
- Root cause: two compounded flaws — the oracle's price `submit()` function was publicly callable, and a weak governance process let the attacker pass a proposal adding FTS as collateral with a 0.7 (700000000000000000) collateral factor, which sat active for 3 days without challenge.
- Mechanics: the attacker funded the attack with ETH sourced from Tornado Cash on mainnet, swapped it for large FTS holdings to both reach governance quorum and back collateral. After the proposal passed, they manipulated the FTS oracle price through the public submit() and deposited just 100 FTS (~$4.5 pre-hack) as collateral against the 0.7 factor — enough to drain every asset on the platform (~$3M). Afterwards they deposited 1,048 ETH (~$2.6M) and 400k DAI into Tornado Cash.
- Lesson: (1) A price feed with any externally callable setter — public submit(), or one controlled by a single EOA — inside a lending platform is an instant full-drain primitive; require multi-source/Chainlink-style validation and access control. (2) Monitor governance parameters: an absurd collateral factor passing quorum and living 3 days unchallenged is a governance alerting failure — set time-locks plus parameter sanity bounds, and alert on suspicious proposals.
- REKT narrative extras: attack decoded by BlockSec and Certik; Fortress is the lending arm of JetFuel Finance; both auditors (Hash0x, EtherAuthority) missed the oracle bug; the site listed ChainLink among "collaboraters" while actually using a manipulable self-reported feed.

## Furucombo — 2021-03-01 — ~$14M
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum — "evil contract" exploit: nested delegatecall chain + storage-slot poisoning, draining users' infinite approvals
- Root cause: the Furucombo proxy made caller-specified delegatecalls to whitelisted handlers; the Aave V2 LendingPool handler was itself a proxy that resolves its implementation from a storage slot — under delegatecall it read that slot from the Furucombo proxy's own storage, where the attacker had already planted their malicious contract (via a prior tx invoking the Aave proxy's initialize() through the FC proxy). The registry whitelist existed but was bypassed by the storage-level override.
- Mechanics: first the attacker used a malicious contract to make the FC proxy delegatecall Aave V2's proxy `initialize()`, writing the attacker's implementation address into the FC proxy's slot. Then fund-stealing txs were sent to the FC proxy with the Aave LendingPool proxy as "handler": the nested delegatecalls resolved to the hacker's code, which ran in the FC proxy's context and transferred away every token users had approved (~$14M across many wallets: 3.9k stETH, 2.4M USDC, 649k USDT, 257k DAI, aTokens/cTokens, 142M BAO, 38.6k PERP and more). Cream Finance's treasury was also drained as a "borrower".
- Lesson: (1) When a proxy delegatecalls a proxy, audit every storage slot the callee reads — an implementation-address slot that shares layout with the caller's storage lets an attacker rewrite the code that runs. Restrict handler functions/parameters and don't let handlers self-modify implementation slots. (2) User-side: infinite approvals turn any single compromised router contract into full-wallet theft — scoped approvals + periodic revocation (Debank/Etherscan revoke tools) is a hunting-relevant hygiene check for integrated apps.
- REKT narrative extras: Kurt Barry traced the nested delegatecalls via ethtx.info; rekt interviewed victim Limzero, who repaid loans at health factor 1.15 and revoked all permissions 90 minutes after the theft; auditor Haechi later disputed scope, noting the exploited Aave-via-proxy scenario was never in their audit scope.
