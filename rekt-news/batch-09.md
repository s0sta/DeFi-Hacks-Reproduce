## Spartan Protocol — 2021-05-02 — $30.5M
- Chain/Attack vector: BSC; flash-loan-funded manipulation of the SPARTA/WBNB pool's LP-burn share calculation (10K WBNB flash loan, 260 WBNB fee).
- Root cause: `calcLiquidityShare()` computed redemption shares from the pool's CURRENT token balances instead of the cached `baseAmountPooled`/`tokenAmountPooled` accounting — so inflating the pool's balances (by swapping in and donating) before burning LP tokens let the attacker redeem far more underlying assets than he had deposited.
- Mechanics: With a 10K WBNB flash loan the attacker swapped WBNB→SPARTA five times (1,913 WBNB each → 2,536,613 SPARTA), added that plus 11,853 WBNB to mint 933,350 SPT1-WBNB LP tokens; then ten more swaps (1,674 WBNB each → 2,639,121 SPARTA) and donated all SPARTA plus 21,632 WBNB straight into the pool to inflate its balances. Burning the 933K LP tokens then withdrew 2,538,199 SPARTA + 20,694 WBNB (≈9K WBNB profit on an 11,853 WBNB deposit), and a second add-1,414,010-LP-then-burn cycle netted 2,643,882 SPARTA + 21,555 WBNB; the loop repeated until the pool was drained, then the 100,260 WBNB loan was repaid. About a third of the profit was lost to slippage on exit: final haul 219.5 BTC (~$12.4M) + 3,311 ETH (~$9.7M), with the bulk parked at 0x3b6e… (30.7K WBNB, 4.6M SPARTA, 1.3M BUSD-T, 23.2 BTCB, 924K BUSD).
- Lesson: Liquidity-share (LP burn/withdraw) math must use cached, cumulative pool accounting — never live `balanceOf`-style reads — because donation/swap-inflated balances turn LP redemptions into free withdrawals. When auditing a fork of an ETH blue chip, diff the share-calculation logic against the original line by line; "optimizations" introduced during the BSC copy-paste boom are where the holes live.
- REKT narrative extras: $30M extracted for $66 in flash-loan fees ("excellent ROI" — rekt); Peckshield's root-cause analysis and Igor Igamberdiev's exfil tracing (1inch → Nerve → Anyswap swaps to BTCB/BETH) are credited. 6th on the leaderboard; rekt wondered aloud how rekt you have to get before CZ steps in.

## Sturdy Finance — 2023-06-12 — ~$800K (442 ETH)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; flash-loan + read-only reentrancy on the SturdyOracle to manipulate the price of collateral token B-stETH-STABLE in a leveraged-yield lending protocol.
- Root cause: The (unfortunately named) SturdyOracle priced collateral from live pool state that could be skewed mid-call by a flash-loan-funded swap/donation — the read-only reentrancy family previously seen at Midas Capital and dForce Network. Balancer forum posts in February 2023 had already flagged the specific pools as susceptible.
- Mechanics: Attacker 0x1e8419e7… deployed attack contract 0x0b09c862… (with built-in front-running protection) and flash-loaned funds to distort the oracle's reported value of B-stETH-STABLE collateral, then borrowed against the inflated collateral, leaving the protocol underwater; profit 442 ETH (~$800K) was deposited into Tornado Cash roughly 20 minutes after the funding transaction. Ancilia raised the alarm and the team paused all markets shortly after.
- Lesson: Read-only reentrancy corrupts price quotes without any classic reentrant call — for every view/pricing function, ask whether its inputs (pool balances, reserves, totalSupply) can change between the quote and the borrow; the answer is the attack window. Also treat vulnerability advisories about the pools you integrate (the Feb 2023 Balancer post named the exact pools) as in-scope audit findings, not somebody else's problem.
- REKT narrative extras: Ancilia spotted it first, BlockSec reconstructed the steps; despite three audits (CertiK, Quantstamp, Code4rena) the vulnerable LendingPool version fell outside audit scope — Quantstamp pointed this out in June 2024 and the leaderboard entry was updated. Rekt tied it to the then-hot "oracle-free lending" debate.

## Sonne Finance — 2024-05-14 — $20M (WETH, VELO, soVELO, wrapped USDC)
- Chain/Attack vector: Optimism; Compound v2 fork donation attack on the brand-new VELO market, executed atomically with the governance proposal that activated it.
- Root cause: The known Compound V2 donation attack (inflating a fresh market's exchange rate by donating tokens, then borrowing against it) plus a governance-process flaw: the proposal setting the VELO collateral factor to 35% was executable by ANYONE once the 24h timelock expired, so the attacker bundled proposal-execution and exploit in one transaction. Sonne's own yAudit had flagged "Unclear protection against Hundred Finance attack vector" as a HIGH finding.
- Mechanics: Sonne deployed the VELO market and created a proposal with a 4-day governance period; after it passed and the timelock expired, the attacker's bot won the race to execute it and, in the same transaction (0x9312ae37…), donated into the soVELO/soUSDC/soWETH markets to inflate exchange rates, then borrowed out ~$20M of WETH, VELO, soVELO and wrapped USDC from ~$60M TVL. Nerv Alert sounded the alarm late Tuesday with an initial $3M estimate before the tally reached $20M; MEV researcher Tony KΞ (fuzzland) front-ran the attacker with just $100 of gas and prevented >$6.5M in additional losses.
- Lesson: A known, warned-about and audit-flagged vulnerability is still exploitable if the governance activation sequence isn't atomic — market creation, collateral-factor setting and donation-defense (virtual/dead shares) must go live as one indivisible operation BEFORE the first user/attacker can touch the new market. For any Compound fork: every "add new market" proposal is a countdown; time the activation window and pre-compute the donation math.
- REKT narrative extras: Hundred Finance had publicly warned other Compound forks after its own 2023 donation attack, and Sonne "turned a deaf ear"; the team posted the exploit on Discord first and X only hours later, with a post-mortem ~5h after the attack. Users spotted that Mendi Finance is a friendly fork of Sonne — rekt's closer: "Could they be exploited soon?"

## Stars Arena — 2023-10-07 — ~$2.9M (entire TVL; plus ~$2K in the first hack)
- Chain/Attack vector: Avalanche; FriendTech clone hit twice in two days — a broken `getPrice()` drain, then a reentrancy in `sellShares()` on the replacement (still unverified) contract.
- Root cause: First hack: broken `getPrice()` let the contract be drained, but gas cost ~4x the profit, so only ~$2K was lost before exploiters gave up. The team then upgraded the proxy to a new, also-unverified contract. Second hack: per Beosin, a reentrancy in `sellShares()` let the attacker insert a block height as the number of AVAX to send — i.e., the payout amount was attacker-controlled.
- Mechanics: On Saturday the attacker reentered `sellShares()` so the contract sent AVAX equal to a (large) block-height value instead of the owed share proceeds, draining ~$2.9M — the protocol's entire TVL. Hacker address: 0xa2Ebf3FCD757e9BE1E58B643b6B5077D11b4ad7A. The team's response to the first incident had been "THE EXPLOIT HAS BEEN FIXED. BUT DON'T GET THIS WRONG WE ARE AT WAR."
- Lesson: An emergency "fix" that upgrades to another unverified contract is not a fix — verify bytecode before and after every upgrade, and specifically audit share-buy/sell functions for reentrancy and for any path where the payout amount can be influenced by calldata or chain state. Treat an unverified, un-audited fast-fork shilled by ecosystem insiders as a risk signal in itself, not as validation.
- REKT narrative extras: Ava Labs CEO Emin Gün Sirer shilled the project for a week, then dismissed the full drain as "a mere speed bump" recoverable "in about 10 days", doubling down as CT watched; the team claimed "security is the core driving factor" while reportedly holding user private keys and being open to DDoS. Analysis credited to 0xlilitch and Beosin; the team floated a relaunch.

## SushiSwap (RouteProcessor2 "Yoink") — 2023-04-09 — $3.3M (across 14 chains; 0xSifu hit for 1,800 ETH)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum + 13 other chains; the 4-day-old RouteProcessor2 router accepted arbitrary route data — a fake UniV3 pool impersonated a liquidity pool to trigger `transferFrom` on any address that had approved the router.
- Root cause: In `processRoute`/`uniswapV3SwapCallback`, the callback did not verify that the calling pool was a genuinely deployed UniV3 pool ("line 328 comment is correct, line 340 does not check the pool deployer" — 0xfoobar), so a no-op swap on an attacker-created fake pool executed `safeTransferFrom` on an arbitrary ERC20 with an arbitrary `from` address — draining every user who had granted the router allowance.
- Mechanics: The attacker created a fake UniV3 pool, passed its address in place of a real liquidity pool in a crafted route, and during the callback the router pulled approved tokens from victims' wallets to the attacker ("yoink"). Over $3.3M was stolen from users who had approved the days-old contract across 14 chains. DeFi antagonist 0xSifu lost 1,800 ETH; BlockSec and other whitehats raced to revoke/front-run user approvals, and one self-described whitehat who targeted Sifu botched the return — only 100 ETH came back. Example tx 0xea3480f1…; router on ETH: 0x044b75f554b886A065b9567891e45c79542d7357.
- Lesson: Every callback invoked by an untrusted external contract must authenticate its caller against a trusted pool set (factory-derived address check), because in a router a fake pool equals arbitrary `transferFrom` on every historical approver. Newly deployed router contracts are prime hunting ground: map each callback for missing `msg.sender` validation and user-controlled pool addresses, and check which big wallets hold stale approvals.
- REKT narrative extras: Head Chef Jared Grey acknowledged the bug, urged revocations, and promised a full post-mortem after removing the exploited contract; credited analysts Inspex, 0xfoobar and ernestognw. Rekt folded it into Sushi's long drama arc (internal infighting, Grey's SEC subpoena and $500K salary, "paltry" bounties) and noted the 4-day-old router's small approval set saved it from becoming "AMM-ageddon".

## Team Finance — 2022-10-27 — $15.8M (4 projects' locked liquidity)
- Chain/Attack vector: Ethereum; flawed `migrate()` in Team Finance's Liquidity Lock "v2→v3 migration" contract — locked Uniswap V2 LP moved into attacker-controlled V3 pairs at skewed prices, with the leftover refunded to the attacker.
- Root cause: The `migrate()` function let a caller migrate locked UniV2 liquidity into a NEW V3 pair with attacker-set (skewed) pricing, producing a huge "leftover" that was refunded as profit; PeckShield noted the authorized-sender check was bypassed simply by locking any tokens. The vulnerable function sat inside the Zokyo-audited (Aug 2022) "bulletproof" lock contracts.
- Mechanics: The attacker created his own UniV3 pairs with manipulated price curves and called the flawed `migrate()` to move each project's real UniV2 locked liquidity into them, pocketing the leftover refund on each migration. Per-pool losses: CAW $11.5M, FEG $1.9M, TSUKA $1.7M (price cushioned by a secondary UniV3 pool), KNDX $0.7M — ~$15.8M total. Exploiter addresses 0x161cebb8… and 0xba399a25… (stolen funds); attack contract 0xcff07c4e…; attack tx 0xb2e3ea72….
- Lesson: Any migrate/upgrade path that moves LP must whitelist its destination pools (price-verified against the source pair) and enforce sender authorization per lock owner — a check that any lock action can satisfy is no check at all. Security-infrastructure providers are themselves attack surface: an "anti-rug" tool with a flawed migration primitive rugs every project it "protects" in one exploit.
- REKT narrative extras: The self-proclaimed "Industry Leader In Project Security & Automation" lost $15.8M of funds it was safeguarding — rekt: "Four projects got rugged through their shared anti-rug mechanism." #46 on the leaderboard; rekt mocked the claim of protecting $2.5B+ in assets given the liquidity profile of the locked tokens.
